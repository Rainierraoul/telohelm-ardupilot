# Paper 3 protocol, third version: which parts of learning with a hazard present help

Draft 3, written on 19 November 2026 by a Claude session from draft 2 (`protocol/drafts/PROTOCOL-3-draft-2.md`) and the rulings taken since it. Every change is listed with its reason in `protocol/drafts/CHANGES-PROTOCOL-3-draft-3.md`. Paths are relative to `paper3/`. This text is not frozen and no scored flight has flown under it.

## What this document is

The protocol is the plan for one experiment, written before the experiment is run and kept unchanged afterwards. Telohelm is a system in which a language model writes a short control procedure for a drone, a monitor flies the procedure in simulation, and the procedure is adopted only if it passes. This study asks which parts of that learning process help when a hazard, a zone the drone must keep out of, is present. It compares four ways of learning (the arms, section 1.1) on three flight tasks, each on fresh flights the model never saw.

The second protocol, `PROTOCOL-2.md`, was frozen on 4 November 2026 and run once (`runs/scored-2`). It stays as it was, with its code and its results. This protocol adds a new hazard study. The paper will report Study 1 from the second protocol and the hazard study from this one.

The text has this order. Sections 1 to 3 say what is asked and what is fixed. Section 4 says how the run is flown. Sections 5 to 7 say how seeds, analysis and faults are handled. Section 8 says what the development work showed and what it changed. Section 9 says what must happen before the freeze, and section 10 says what the study cannot show. A glossary follows this introduction.

## Glossary

A reader who knows drones but not this project can use these as they come. Each term is also explained where it first matters.

- Arm. One way of learning within a block. There are four, A to D (section 1.1).
- Block. One task's set of 3 learning layouts, on which all four arms learn.
- Candidate. One procedure the model writes. c0 and c1 are first candidates, f1 and f2 fresh ones, rC1, rC2, rD1 and rD2 repairs.
- Layout, scenario. One flight set-up: a target and, in the threat version, a zone. Learning layouts and evaluation scenarios have no wind; only five of each task's ten retention scenarios do.
- Zone. A circular area the drone must keep out of, which switches on during the flight.
- Trial. The 3 layouts a candidate must fly, and pass, before it is adopted.
- Adopted. A candidate passed its trial and the arm uses it from then on, with no model.
- Telemetry step. 0.1 s. The vehicle reports its state ten times a second.
- Decision, own lag. A decision is one step of the procedure. Its own lag is the simulated time from its observation to the moment its command is ready.
- Fixed delay. Every command is sent to the vehicle 0.3 s after the observation it was computed from, three telemetry steps (section 4.6).
- Deadline. A decision is ready in time if its own lag is under three telemetry steps. Otherwise the flight is late.
- Host lag, clock offset. A host lag is an observation read one step after its schedule because the decision's turnaround crossed the next telemetry stamp. A clock offset is the same late reading when the stamp nearest the scheduled time falls a millisecond or two before it, which is common in a process's first flight (sections 4.6 and 8.5).
- Free check. A flown check of the platform, with the reference controllers and two scripted procedures and no model call, made with the delay and without it (section 9.1).
- Scripted procedures, scripted replies. Procedures the model wrote in scored-2, kept in a file and given back in place of a model call, so a pilot flies the whole study with no model.
- Pilot, rehearsal. The scripted pilot flies one block per task with scripted replies. A rehearsal is the whole chain of section 9.1 flown on the archive the sample will use. They are named by job number in section 8.10.
- Chain. The batch job of section 9.1 that flies the free checks, the pilot, the checks of them and then the development sample.
- Position test. A diagnostic of when decisions became ready. In a learning block's trial flights it counts the decisions whose observation was stamped on time and which became ready one telemetry step or more after their scheduled time. It divides that count by all decisions after each flight's first, and compares the first third of the flights with the last third. The block fails if the share rises by more than 23.2 percentage points, the largest rise among scored-2's processes that did not reach a second repair. It is reported and does not gate (round four, 'The position test'; section 8.4).
- F0, F1. Names of the first free tests of the platform. F0 is the package's containment tests, run on each node. F1 is the free check without the delay (section 8.8).
- Fingerprint. One SHA-256 hash over all executed source (section 2).
- Gate. The program that checks a whole run for well-formed records, timing, platform and rules before any outcome is computed (section 6.7).
- Set aside. A phase's folder and log moved out of the run so that the phase can be flown again under its own name, with a receipt (section 7.3).
- Held-out, evaluation flights. Fresh flights, flown with learning off, that the learning blocks never saw.
- Primary and secondary intervals. The three primary intervals are the pooled steps B − A, C − B and D − C, whose names answer RQ1 to RQ3 and on which the sample size was planned. Every other interval is secondary, among them the six per-task D − A intervals that answer RQ4 (section 6.3).
- Scored-2. The second protocol's run.
- Ruling. A numbered decision recorded in the note.
- Build, part. A build is one batch of code changes made by one builder and reviewed as a unit, named by a letter. A part is one batch of a second series of code jobs, lettered D to L, which covered the gate, the analysis, the set-aside tool, the chain and the batch scripts. Some later batches are called builds again (E for `--earlier`, M and N), so a letter is always cited with its ruling.
- Phase. One scheduled job of the scheduler: a learning block (`learn3`), an evaluation of zone flights (`threat3`) or of retention flights (`retain3`).
- Learner. One arm's sequence of candidates and trials within a block.
- Owner. The Claude session holding the protocol-3 work at the time; since 18 November the coordinating session ("Who decided what").

## 1. Questions

Telohelm is a system in which a language model writes a short control procedure, a monitor flies the procedure in simulation, and the procedure is adopted only if it passes. An adopted procedure then flies with no model. The vehicle is a copter flown by ArduPilot in its software-in-the-loop simulator (SITL), as in the second protocol.

A threat zone is a circular area the drone must keep out of, which switches on during the flight. Entering it fails the flight. The task text tells the model that zones may appear, in every condition of this study (`SHARED_GOAL` in `ardupilot/pack.py`).

The second protocol found that procedures learned with a zone present met the goal on fresh zone flights more often than procedures learned on the same layouts with the zone removed. The differences were 0.681 for reach, 0.313 for reach3d and 0.506 for land, with 8 pairs of learning runs per task and wide intervals (`manuscript/PAPER.md`:5). That comparison changed two things together: whether a candidate's adoption test flew the zone, and whether the flights shown to the model before its first attempt had the zone in them. Its arms did not differ in the evidence after a failure as a separate part, and only the arm with the zone ever sent a repair. Most targets also lay about 3 m past the zone. The repair of that study showed the model layouts flown again with no procedure in control, which is not what a repair shows in this protocol (section 2.2).

This protocol asks one question in three parts, and a fourth about distance. The questions keep their numbers, which the protocol was fixed with.

- RQ1, testing (B − A). Hold the first candidate and the fresh candidates the same. Does testing candidates against the zone before adoption, rather than on the same layouts without it, change how often a learning block supplies a procedure that meets the goal on fresh zone flights?
- RQ2, repair (C − B), the main question. With the same first candidate, the same zone test and the same number of candidates allowed, does repairing a candidate that failed the test, rather than drawing a fresh candidate from the original request, change that rate? The primary C − B is taken over all blocks, including those in which the first candidate passed and arms B and C were the same arm. The comparison within blocks whose first candidate failed is a secondary estimate.
- RQ3, first examples (D − C). With testing against the zone and repair already in place, does showing the zone in the flights the model sees before its first candidate change that rate?
- RQ4, distance (D − A by band). Does the total difference between arm D and arm A hold for targets 9 to 21 m beyond the zone as well as for targets 3 to 4 m beyond it? It is answered for each task by two secondary intervals, D − A on close targets and D − A on distant ones. A target further from the zone is also further from home, so the bands differ in mission length too, and the paper never puts a band difference down to clearance alone.

RQ2 is the main question because it has a published counterpart and because it is the choice a designer faces once a test is in place, between paying for repair and paying for more draws. The results are reported with C − B first, then the others (section 6.3). No direction is promised for any step.

Which intervals are primary. RQ1 to RQ3 are each answered by one primary interval, a step pooled over the three tasks: B − A, C − B and D − C. These three are the intervals the sample size was planned for, and they lead the paper's answer. RQ4 is answered by six secondary intervals, close and distant D − A for each task. They are computed from the same draws as the primary intervals, named by the same rules and reported in full (section 6.3).

The six were primary until 19 November. The planning simulation was then corrected to agree with the second development sample. It showed that arm D's gain over arm A within one task is often smaller than the margin, and that 40 blocks of one task cannot name a gain that size. Simon therefore made the six secondary, before any scored outcome existed (section 6.6; ruling 88). The plan fixed before the development sample did not allow this change, so it is reported as a departure (section 8.4). The protocol says now, before the run, that the study cannot promise to resolve the six, and that an unresolved interval is not evidence of no difference.

Success always means the same thing: a learning run adopted a procedure, and that procedure met the goal on a fresh zone flight. A run that adopts nothing has not supplied a working procedure, so it counts as a failure on every flight.

### 1.1 The four arms

These terms are used throughout.

- A layout is one flight set-up: its target and its zone, with no wind. A layout is flown in two versions. The threat version has the zone. The matched version is the same layout with the zone removed.
- A block is one task's set of 3 learning layouts. Every arm of a block learns on the same 3 layouts.
- A candidate is one procedure the model writes.
- A trial list is the 3 layouts a candidate must fly, in the threat or the matched version. A candidate is adopted only if it passes on all 3 (section 2).
- An arm is one way of learning within a block. There are four.

Each block makes two first requests to the model. Before either, the block's layouts are flown with no procedure in control, so the drone holds its position and does not meet the goal. These are the first-round flights, and moments from them are the examples a first request shows. P0 is the first request whose examples come from the matched first-round flights. P1 is the first request whose examples come from the threat first-round flights, in which the zone switches on at half the time limit. The candidate written from P0 is called c0, and the candidate from P1 is c1.

| Arm | First candidate | Trial list | After a failed trial |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | c0 | matched | a fresh candidate from P0, then one more |
| B | c0 | threat | a fresh candidate from P0, then one more |
| C | c0 | threat | a repair, then a repair of that |
| D | c1 | threat | a repair, then a repair of that |

A fresh candidate is the reply to P0 sent again, byte for byte, with nothing about the failure in it. Arms A and B draw their fresh candidates from one stream for the block, called f1 and f2, and each arm tests a fresh candidate on its own trial list. A repair is Telohelm's own next attempt. Its request carries the refused candidate's source, a reason for each flight that failed, and moments from the failed flights of the trial just flown (section 2.2). The repairs are called rC1 and rC2 in arm C, and rD1 and rD2 in arm D.

Arms B and C share c0 and its threat trial. If c0 passes that trial, both adopt c0 and are identical. They part only when c0 fails.

### 1.2 What differs between neighbouring arms

Each research question is one step between two arms.

- B − A answers RQ1. The two arms have the same first candidate and the same fresh candidates. Only the trial list differs: A tests on the matched layouts and B on the threat layouts.
- C − B answers RQ2. The two arms have the same first candidate, the same trial list and the same number of candidate slots. They differ in what follows a failed trial: C repairs and B samples afresh.
- D − C answers RQ3. Both arms test on the threat layouts and repair. They differ in the first candidate: D's is written from flights that show the zone.
- D − A is the total, and answers RQ4 when it is read for each task separately for close and for distant targets.

The steps are comparisons in one fixed order, the order in which the parts would be added in practice (DESIGN:38 and 115). They are not the main effects of a factorial design. Seeing the zone without being tested against it is not an arm, and the interactions left out are not estimated.

Three limits belong with these contrasts and are repeated in section 10.

- In the corrected planning simulation, arm B adopts nothing in 60.0% to 98.5% of blocks, in each of the three tasks (section 6.6). In the development sample it adopted in 2 of 12 blocks (section 8.4). In scored-2 every reach procedure learned without the zone failed with the target 3 m past it, so a fresh candidate from P0 rarely passes a trial that includes a 3 m layout. If the scored run agrees, C − B is close to arm C's own success rate, and B − A measures what a strict test costs when nothing repairs the failures.
- Arm C is the first arm in which the model sees any flight with a zone in it. C − B therefore carries two things together: the evidence in the failed zone flights, and the revision of the failed candidate. This protocol does not separate them. The published comparison this step follows has the same form. In Olausson et al. (2024), the repair step is conditioned on the specification, the failed program, its error message and feedback on it (their section 3.1). Their baseline 'is simply i.i.d. sampling from the corresponding model' (their section 4), and the two are compared at an equal number of samples or tokens (their section 3.2 and Appendix A).
- A step pooled over the three tasks can hide effects that point in opposite directions (section 6.4).

### 2.2 What each request shows the model

What a request shows is fixed by Telohelm's unchanged core, and this section is the definition. It was read from the core's code (`telohelm/examples.py` and `telohelm/learning.py` in the wheel) and from the text of requests built offline with a marker in every flight (`ardupilot/test_ladder.py`).

A request holds four things:

- the reasons, one for each flight that failed since the learner's previous request;
- example moments from those failed flights;
- a list of the learner's earlier refused attempts;
- the guard's source, with the names of the fields a procedure can read.

Two of these need defining.

- Examples come only from the failed flights named in the reasons. Each flight gives 3 moments: halfway, three-quarters of the way through and its last second. At most 7 are shown, the newest flight first. If an earlier flight of the same learner met its goal, moments from the most recent such flight are added as a contrast, to at most 8 in all. If the request is too long for the input limit, the oldest moments are dropped first.
- The earlier attempts are the last 3, each with the stage that refused it and a one-line reason. Only the most recent carries its source.

Every request also carries the task's text, and in this protocol two things are added to it, the same in every task and arm.

- One sentence of fact about the fixed delay of section 4.6, with no advice on what to do about it. It reads: 'Each command you return is sent to the drone 0.3 s after the observation it was computed from; until then the drone keeps flying on the previous command.' Its number is read from `COMMAND_DELAY_S`. The sentence is added to a copy of the task's pack, so the second protocol's requests do not change (`delay_sentence` and `protocol3_pack` in `ardupilot/ladder.py`). No request carries a time or a lag field (round four, ruling 6; round five, decision 5). The model is told because the free check showed that the delay changes how procedures written without it fly near a zone. Left untold, the delay would be something that arms with richer feedback could find out and others could not, a difference between arms that the design did not plan.
- One sentence asking for a procedure only: 'In this run reply with one entry of kind "procedure" only. Checks and pairs are not accepted, whatever the rules above say about them.' (`PROCEDURE_ONLY` in `ardupilot/pack.py`.) It sits in the system text after the core's own rules and before the core's example rules, so it does not end the request. The core offers checks and pairs whatever the task allows. Without the sentence 49 of the first sample's 64 candidate slots spent their second call repairing a reply the task never accepts, and the refusals fell unequally on the kinds of candidate (section 8.11). Scored-2's learner did not have the sentence, so this departs from it on purpose (ruling 37, point 9).

Every request for a reply also carries a body field, `"cache": {"no-cache": true, "no-store": true}`, which tells the gateway neither to answer from its store nor to store the reply. Token-count requests do not carry it, since they return a number and not a reply (ruling 45, point 5). A reply whose message id the run has already seen is a copy, and the provider does not pass it on as an answer (section 7.1).

The leak inspection found every request at this delay to be the text it had read before with only the sentence's number changed. It found no seed number or held-out result in a request, and asked that this section say that every request carries the delay sentence (ruling 25). It is run again at each change of the fingerprint (section 4.8).

| Request | Reasons and examples come from | Earlier attempts listed |
|---|---|---|
| c0, f1, f2 | the 3 matched first-round flights | none |
| c1 | the 3 threat first-round flights | none |
| rC1 | c0's failed threat trial flights | c0, with its source |
| rC2 | rC1's failed threat trial flights | c0 and rC1, with rC1's source |
| rD1 | c1's failed threat trial flights | c1, with its source |
| rD2 | rD1's failed threat trial flights | c1 and rD1, with rD1's source |

Three things follow. A repair does not show the first-round flights again, because they were the reasons for the first request and not for this one. A second repair shows the flights of the candidate just before it, and what it keeps of the first candidate's failure is a one-line reason. If the candidate just before was an invalid reply and never flew, the reasons and examples are those of the trial before it. If no candidate of the arm has flown yet, because every earlier reply was invalid, they are still those of the first request, and the repair shows the arm's own first-round flights.

C3's contract describes a repair this way (CONTRACT:18–19). D3's schema adds 'their corresponding initial examples' (SCHEMA:26), which the core does not show. The owner ruled that the driver stands. The core that writes the requests is Telohelm's own and is unchanged. What the driver gives it differs from what the second protocol's driver gave it. There, a repair after a failed test showed layouts flown again with no procedure in control (`procedure_steps` is 0 in all 33 such flights of scored-2's 48 learning runs). Here a repair shows the failed candidate's own zone-trial flights. The pilot request `cluster-20261117/chain-F5-4800755/runs/pilot3-scripted-20261117-F5/learn3-reach3d-b0/candidates/rD1/request.json` lists as its shown flights candidate c1's two threat trials (ruling 71, point 2).

For the contrasts this means the following. C − B compares a candidate written from the failed zone trial, with the refused source and the reasons, against a candidate written again from the matched first-round flights. After the first candidate, arms C and D build their repairs in the same way from their own failed zone trials, and neither is shown first-round flights again. D − C therefore differs in the first candidate alone and in what follows from it. The zone in P1's first-round flights reaches the model through c1 and through nothing else.

The driver keeps two lists for every request and every call. `context_flights` holds every flight the learner had seen, which is more than a request shows. `shown_flights` holds the flights whose moments the text kept, taken from the core's own record of what it kept after any dropping. The gate checks the second list: a first or fresh request shows only its own first-round flights, and a repair shows only trial flights of its own arm, with at least one from the last trial flown. A repair before any flown trial may show only its own first-round flights.

## 3. Tasks and scenarios

The tasks are reach, reach3d and land, as the second protocol defines them (`PROTOCOL-2.md`, section 3). Reach flies to a point at fixed height and holds there. Reach3d does the same with a target height. Land comes down on a point slowly enough.

A scenario is one flight set-up. Its zone and the rule that switches the zone on are those of the second protocol's threat family for the same seed. The bearing of the zone and target from home is drawn evenly from all directions. Learning layouts and evaluation scenarios have no wind: `generate` in `ardupilot/battle.py` sets the wind to 0 for every family except the windy retention one, and the scenario's last field, the wind's direction, then does nothing. Wind applies only to the five windy retention scenarios of each task. There the wind is the wind set in the simulator, and ArduPilot's simulator scales it by the square root of height over 60 m when the vehicle is below 60 m (`SIM_WIND_T` 0 and `SIM_WIND_T_ALT` 60, which nothing in this study overrides). At 10 m the drone therefore meets about 0.41 of the set speed, and at 20 m about 0.58. This holds for every flight with wind, which here means the windy retention flights (ruling 92, point 4). The zone lies across the straight path and switches on at the first step that begins with the drone within 19 m of its near edge, or at half the time limit if that has not happened.

One thing is new. The target is placed at a set clearance beyond the zone, on the line from home through the zone's centre. Its distance from home is the distance of the zone's centre, plus the zone's radius, plus the clearance (`generate` in `ardupilot/battle.py`:88–107, with `gap_stratum`). The clearance is drawn evenly from one of three bands: close, 3 to 4 m; middle, 9 to 11 m; wide, 19 to 21 m [PICKED 3]. Without the option the generator gives exactly the second protocol's scenarios. All 667 of its reserved scenarios were compared and matched (`reproduction/protocol3-generator-regression-20261108.json`).

A target that is further from the zone is also further from home, so the flight is longer. The bands are therefore families of geometry. A difference between bands cannot be put down to clearance alone (DESIGN:129).

The matched version of a scenario has the same target and no zone. Episode names such as `threat-close-<seed>` never reach the model. The driver names every flight a request mentions by its alias, in all four arms, and the second protocol's checks of the request apply (`PROTOCOL-2.md`, section 3). The gate searches every request for the seed numbers of both seed books, the learning seeds included (section 6.7). Until 13 November 2026 a repair request of arm C named its failed trial layout by its raw name and seed; the leak inspection found it (`notes/LEAK-INSPECTION-20261112.md`, finding 1) and the driver was changed before the development sample.

## 4. Design

### 4.1 Learning

Each task has 40 blocks, numbered 0 to 39. A block's 3 layouts have one seed in each band (`blocks[task][b]` in `protocol/seeds-3.json`). One learning phase, `learn3`, runs the whole block in one process:

1. It flies the 3 matched and the 3 threat first-round flights.
2. It asks the model for c0 from P0 and for c1 from P1.
3. It runs the four arms. A fresh candidate or a repair is requested only when an arm needs it.
4. It writes the block's records (section 4.4).

A block flies in calls: the first-round flights of each list, then one candidate's trial on one list at a time. If any flight of a call is late (section 4.6), the block moves that call's folder, before anything reads it, to `late/<the call's path>/attempt-<k>` inside the block folder. It then flies the same source on the same cases again at once, in the same process, with no model call. It does this at most twice [PICKED 9]. If the third attempt is late as well, it is kept: the block reads it as it reads any flight, and its rows in `timing.json` are marked `kept_late`. The sandbox allows a procedure 2 s of processor time a decision, so a procedure written by a model could be slow enough to miss its deadline at every attempt. Keeping the third attempt is how its slowness is charged to it (round two, ruling 3; round four, ruling 2). No block is set aside for a late flight, so nothing can choose blocks by how many repairs they needed. A late first-round or trial flight is set aside before any request uses it. The first-round flights have no procedure in control and make no model call, and they go through the same rule. If the third attempt of a first-round call is late it is kept and marked kept late. No learned procedure flew it, so there is nothing to charge it to. The analysis counts it with the arms whose first request showed its flights: the matched list (P0) with arms A, B and C, and the threat list (P1) with arm D (`arms_of_call` in `protocol/analysis3.py`).

A model call can fail in transport, time out, be refused by the gateway, come back with no served-model label or be a copy of a reply the run has already seen. Then the block stops at once and is a fault, set aside and flown again by the rule of section 7.1. Every reply the service gives names the model that served it, so a call without that label was not answered by a model. It never uses up a candidate slot, and a finished block never holds one. A reply that is empty or breaks the format rules is the model's answer. It is recorded and uses its slot. A reply with text that breaks the format gets one format-repair call. An empty reply, or one refused or cut off, gets none. A reply that was cut off or did not end normally arrives with no text (`ardupilot/claude_provider.py`:261–266), so it counts as empty. A repair would send the model service an empty assistant turn, which it is likely to refuse (13 November 2026; `notes/LEAK-INSPECTION-20261112.md`, finding 2).

The four arms run in one sitting, so no arm of a block learns at a different time from the others.

### 4.2 Evaluation on fresh zone flights

After learning, each distinct adopted source in a block flies that task's 24 evaluation scenarios, 8 in each band, with learning off and no model available (phase `threat3`). A source is the text of a procedure, identified by its SHA-256. The block's c0 flies them too, even if no arm adopted it, to give the success of a single unrevised attempt.

Arms of one block that adopted the same source share its flights: the source flies each scenario once and the outcome counts for each of those arms. This coupling is fixed here in advance. It does not rest on any claim that the simulator repeats a flight exactly. Two blocks never share flights, even if their sources are identical (CONTRACT:54).

Each evaluation phase flies its scheduled cases in the order set for its simulator instance (`flight_order` in `protocol/contract3.py`; round two, ruling 2). Simulator k of the four starts k quarters of the way down the list and wraps round, so that no two simulators fly the same case at the same moment. The manifest records the order flown, and the gate checks it against the instance. Order cannot change a flight's inputs. Each flight starts a new simulator, flying does not change the library, every decision runs in a fresh sandboxed process and each flight's episode memory is new (round two, ruling 2). A late evaluation phase is set aside and flown again by the rule of section 7.2.

### 4.3 Retention on ordinary flights

Each distinct adopted source also flies 10 ordinary held-out scenarios with no zone, 5 calm and 5 in steady wind, scaled with height as section 3 says (phase `retain3`). This is a secondary check that learning with the zone did not cost ordinary flying. With 10 scenarios and no stated margin it cannot show that nothing was lost.

### 4.4 Records

A learning block's folder holds:

- its manifest, which also names the platform and the sandbox route (sections 4.7 and 4.8);
- `arms.json`, which records for each arm whether it adopted, the adopted candidate and its source hash, the candidates tried in order and each trial's outcome on each layout;
- one folder for each candidate, with its source, its request, the flights the request showed and a receipt for every model call;
- the first-round and trial flights, with their hash-chained logs;
- `late/`, the set-aside attempts of any call flown again for lateness (section 4.1), each with its flights and their logs;
- `timing.json`, with a row for every flight of every attempt (round two, ruling 3). Each row names the call and the attempt and gives the flight's timing fields (section 4.6), whether it was kept late, and the block process's peak resident size;
- `warmup/`, the process's warm-up flight and its timing (section 4.6). An evaluation phase's folder has one too. It is not a flight of the study.
- `usage.jsonl`, with one row for each model call;
- `sandbox-end.json`, the sandbox route's state when the phase ended: its count of calls and every replacement of its holder (section 4.8). An evaluation phase's folder has one too.

The run root also holds `message-ids.jsonl`, the one file of message ids that the provider claims each reply's id in (section 2.2), and the scheduler's `logs/`. An evaluation phase kept late on its third attempt holds `kept-late.json` in its own folder, which names its late flights and the attempts set aside for lateness before it (`KEPT_LATE_FILE` in `ardupilot/scored.py`).

A hash-chained log is a record in which each line carries the hash of the line before, so an edit shows. The layout of these records is in CONTRACT:42–64 and SCHEMA:9–28, as amended by the rule in the takeover note for fields the schema does not name.

### 4.5 Order, pausing and spending

The scored run is flown as three jobs and then one combine step. Each job flies one task's blocks and evaluation on its own node, and nothing else. The combine step runs the gate and the analysis over the three jobs' records as one run. The split is Simon's choice (ruling 52, point 3). The three jobs fly side by side, so the run takes as long as its slowest job (section 8.4 gives the forecast from the second sample's phase times). The split also keeps every within-block comparison on one node, because the four arms of a block still learn and fly together in one process.

The three jobs.

- Each job is one batch job, `cluster/scored.sbatch`, on one L40S node of the gpu partition, for one task: reach, reach3d or land. The three jobs fly at once, each pinned to its own node, so no two share a node. A job refuses to start, and writes nothing, if another job of the same user is running on its node or if a simulator port of the study is already in use (ruling 63, point 4, on finding K1 of the review of part K; ruling 65).
- Each job has its own spending stop of US$20 at list price, given with `--spend-limit 20`. The three stops add up to the US$60 Simon approved (ruling 52, point 3; ruling 42). The script refuses a stop above US$20, and the gate, when the combine step gives it the three roots together, refuses a root whose stop was not set by the flag or is above 20 (`SPLIT_STOP_USD` in `protocol/validate3.py`).
- Each job's run folder and saved copy carry the task in their names. The working folder in memory is named by user, run tag and task and never by the job number, so a resume builds the same paths the first job fixed in the schedule (ruling 66). The scheduler takes `--tasks`, which keeps one task's blocks in the order that `task_order` draws for all three tasks, so the order is the same as it would have been in one run.
- A job saves its run files to shared storage every two minutes and when it ends, and each copy is followed by a list of the files' hashes. The working copy lives in the node's memory while the job flies (section 4.7).
- If a job dies, the same script is submitted again with the dead job's number. It checks with the batch system that the dead job has ended. It then waits until the job's end lies at least KillWait plus UnkillableStepTimeout in the past, the batch system's own limits for stopping a job's processes. It reads both from `scontrol show config` and uses a value only when its number is followed by a space or the end of the line. If either cannot be read that way, or the query fails, it waits 210 s, their sum on this cluster (`kill_wait` in `cluster/scored.sbatch`; rulings 63, 69 and 74). It checks that the saved copy is whole against its hash list, refuses if it is not, and restores the files. It sets aside as `crashed` each phase that had started and not ended, and the scheduler resumes under a recorded reason. A learning block that ended after the last save is lost with the job and is flown again with new model calls. The log names each such block, and the paper reports the number (ruling 39, point 7; rulings 53, 58, 63, 66 and 69).
- Whether to resume is decided by the dead job's end state and by nothing in the run's records. No one reads outcomes to decide.
- Which task flew on which node, the pauses, the resumes and the blocks lost with a dead job are read from each run's own records by `protocol/run_records3.py`, and the paper reports them (rulings 55 and 60). No ruling fixes the assignment of tasks to nodes. The three jobs are submitted pinned to three different nodes.
- Before the scored run, free scripted pilots of this arrangement flew as three jobs of one task each, with one job killed part way and resumed. The third passed, resume and combine step included (ruling 68). The fourth, on the code with the last fix to the resume, passed as well (ruling 84, point 1). They are not of record.

The combine step, `cluster/scored_combine.sbatch`, is free. It takes the three saved folders after checking each against its hash list. It reserves itself with a folder that only one job can make, runs the gate on the three roots as one run and runs the analysis once. It then copies into each root's saved folder the record that the analysis ran, so that a second combine step is refused (rulings 53 and 58). The gate takes the three roots as one run when each holds one task and the three tasks are different. They must also share one source fingerprint, one seed book, one protocol hash and one set of frozen values. A message id that appears in two roots is refused.

The schedule inside a job.

- Learning comes first: the task's blocks in numerical order. Four workers, one for each simulator instance, take phases from the queue in that order, and no more than the number set by `--at-once` fly at a time (section 2). Phases are launched at least 0.25 s apart, so neighbours in the queue fly at the same time and the moment each starts depends on which worker is free. The scheduler writes the queue to `logs/schedule.json` at the first start and refuses a later session whose queue differs or which asks for another number at once.
- Evaluation follows in the same order of blocks: for each finished block, a `threat3` phase for every source it must fly and a `retain3` phase for every adopted source.
- A phase that fails does not stop the scheduler. It holds that phase, lists it and goes on, and evaluation is planned from the blocks that finished.
- The gate and the analysis run once, in the combine step, after all three jobs have ended (section 6).

The order is fixed before launch so that all model use falls as early and as close together as the machine allows, and so that the flights left after learning need no model.

Pausing. A pause may be asked for at any time. It takes effect at a phase boundary, so a learning block is never split. A pause may be asked for because the machine is needed or because of a fault. It may never be asked for because of anything the study has measured: no outcome, no adoption count and no tally of any kind. The scheduler writes each start, request for a pause, pause taking effect and resume to `logs/pauses.jsonl` with its time and reason, and the paper reports how many there were and when. A resume uses the frozen code, seeds, protocol file and model settings, and the gate requires one value of each across the whole run (section 6.7). Pausing adds no block and removes none. If the model label in the replies changes between sittings, the paper reports it. A change of label stops nothing, sets nothing aside and drops nothing, so no one has a decision to make after seeing it.

While a task job is running, nobody runs the gate or the analysis or tabulates outcomes. The people and agents who operate the run read the scheduler's log, exit codes and timing fields. A phase log is opened only for a phase that failed. Operators can see two things that an outcome table would also show: the scheduler log names a `retain3` phase for each adopted source, and the spend rises when an arm adopts late. That cannot be removed without blinding the people who keep the run alive. Section 7.3 removes the decisions an operator could make on what they see, and section 10 names this as a limit.

A partial run is never analysed as this study's result (section 7.4).

Spending. Model cost is counted at the provider's list price from the recorded token counts. The gateway charged 1.10 times list on every call of the second sample (section 8.4), so the cost the project pays is 1.10 times what the stops count.

The stop is a test made when a block is about to start. It counts the rows on disk of finished blocks and of blocks in flight, and a half-written last row is skipped. It counts every call the provider logged once, a copy and a call that ended in a fault included (rulings 39 and 45). Blocks already in flight go on spending after the stop trips. With four at once that is up to three blocks in each job, each of up to 16 calls. So the stop is not a ceiling, and every approval of it is worded as an expected figure and a worst case. A process killed within microseconds of a call can leave one call's cost uncounted by the stop. The gate pairs the provider's rows with the spending rows and reports any such call and its cost, and the paper reports it if it happens (rulings 50 and 58).

The second development sample cost US$3.1701 at list price for 82 calls in 12 blocks, which is about US$0.264 a block, and the gateway charged US$3.4872 for it, about US$0.291 a block (section 8.4). The scored run has 3 × 40 = 120 blocks, so at that rate it is forecast to cost about US$31.70 at list price and about US$34.87 as charged, which ruling 88 rounds to US$35. Each task's job of 40 blocks is forecast at about US$10.57 at list price, against its stop of US$20, and about US$11.62 as charged (ruling 88, point 3). Simon approved about US$40 as charged if N is 40. He also approved a stop of US$60 at list price and a worst case of about US$70 as charged. The worst case is above the stop because blocks in flight finish after a stop trips. A spend above about US$70, a second scored run or a change to the stops goes back to him (ruling 42, point 1). If a job's stop trips, the blocks it has not started do not fly and the run has no gated result (section 7.4). Finishing it would need a deviation that changes frozen code, because both `cluster/scored.sbatch` and the gate (`SPLIT_STOP_USD` in `protocol/validate3.py`) refuse a stop above US$20. Only Simon can decide it, and the decision is taken from the spend record and the count of blocks flown without reading any outcome. Because the split run's stops are US$20, the gate's list of sessions puts every one under `other`, against the single-run stop of US$60; that is expected. Every session's start record carries the stop in force and whether the flag set it. The gate reports every session's stop and the analysis prints them.

The provider's table of list prices gives a cache-read price of US$0.20 per million tokens for Sonnet, where one reading of the price page gave US$0.10. The study sends the gateway an instruction not to use its cache (section 2.2), so no cost uses this price (ruling 52, point 4).

The sample's own limit was `--spend-limit 6`, given on the submit line, and Simon approved the sample's money in ruling 41. Before the sample's first calls and again before its paid step, the chain checks the model key (section 9.1). If replies come back while the spend record stays at zero or empty, the session cancels the job (ruling 28, point 5).

### 4.6 Timing: the fixed delay and its deadline

Decision lag is the simulated time from a step's scheduled observation to the moment its command is ready. A late decision changes what the vehicle does. On 9 November 2026, with the machine heavily loaded, decisions arrived more than 4 s late. A procedure that had met its goal on the same scenario in 3 earlier flights then entered the zone (`reproduction/dev-timing-c3-20261109.json`).

On 13 November the design was changed, at Simon's direction, so that the time a decision takes does not change when its command is sent, unless it takes longer than a fixed time. Every command is applied at a fixed time after the observation it was computed from (the note, round four, opening and ruling 1). That time is three telemetry periods, 0.3 s of simulated time, in every flight of this protocol, in every arm, task and phase, warm-ups included.

How the hold works. Telemetry arrives ten times a second. The episode keeps the simulated time at which each observation was read: the start for the first decision, and for later ones the clock when the step before ended. Once the procedure and the guard have returned a command, the episode reads the telemetry waiting on the link. If the clock is still less than three telemetry steps after the observation, it reads on until the clock is at least the observation plus 0.295 s and then sends the command. So the command goes out just after the third telemetry stamp after the observation. The wait stops 0.005 s short of 0.3 s, as every threshold on a telemetry step is read, so a stamp 1 ms early cannot slip a whole step. If the clock is already three steps or more past the observation when the decision is ready, the deadline was missed: the command goes out at once and the flight is late (`Episode.step` in `ardupilot/pack.py`; round four, ruling 1). After each command the runner waits, as in the second protocol, until the first stamp at or after the next scheduled time, the start plus one second for each step, and takes the next observation there. The simulator does not wait while Telohelm decides. The delay is a setting of `battle.run`, off unless given, so the second protocol's code paths fly as before.

What each step records. Beside what it recorded before, each step of a flight flown with the delay records three times.

- `observation_lag_s`, the observation's simulated time less its schedule.
- `command_lag_s`, the simulated time from the observation to the command going out, which is 0.298 to 0.301 s when the command went out on the deadline's stamp.
- `decision_lag_s`, as before, the time from the schedule to the moment the decision was ready, so that it compares with the older records.

A decision's own lag is `decision_lag_s` less `observation_lag_s`. The outcome records `command_delay_s`, 0.3, and the flight's `run-config.json` records the number of periods (round four, ruling 1).

Why the deadline is counted from the observation. In every long flight on record, scored-2's included, the observation itself is read a step after its schedule in 49% to 60% of decisions from about decision 75 on, against 0% to 3% before it. The host waits until the simulator's clock is at least the scheduled time, and from then on the stamp that should meet it fails the comparison, so the host takes the next one, 0.1 s later. A likely cause, not shown, is floating-point rounding once the start time plus t passes 128 s (round four, 'What the records show', point 2). Counted from the schedule, every two-step lag in the Mac's records came from this pattern. Counted from the observation, none did (point 3). The scheduled wait itself was left as it is, because every record the free check compares against has this pattern, and the paper discloses it (round four, ruling 1).

What a late flight is. A flight of this protocol is late if any of these holds (`timing` in `ardupilot/ladder.py`; round four, ruling 2):

- it does not record `command_delay_s` of 0.3;
- it has no steps, or a step lacks a finite `decision_lag_s`, `observation_lag_s` or `command_lag_s`;
- any decision's own lag reads three telemetry steps or more, which is a missed deadline: the decision was ready 0.295 s or more after its observation;
- any decision that was ready in time had its command go out at other than three steps. This should never happen. It would mean that a telemetry stamp was skipped during the hold.

Each lag is read on the step from 0.005 s below it, as the second protocol's frozen analysis reads its threshold (`protocol/analysis.py`:349; `telemetry_steps` in `ardupilot/ladder.py`). So 0.098, 0.1 and 0.101 s are one step, and 0.0 and 0.001 s are none. The driver, the evaluation phases, the gate and the set-aside tool all apply the one function, so they cannot disagree. Draft 1's rule, a median lag above half a step or any command 1 s or more behind its schedule, no longer applies to this protocol's flights. It is kept as `timing_before_delay` for reading the records flown before 13 November, and the timing preflight tool uses it (round four, ruling 2; section 8.5).

What follows from a late flight. In a learning block the call is flown again, at most twice, and a third late attempt is kept (section 4.1). An evaluation phase with a late flight is set aside and flown again from its start, at most twice, and the third attempt is kept (section 7.2). A missed deadline is rare but not ruled out. On the platform of record a decision takes about 100 ms, so it has to wait about 195 ms more than usual to miss (ruling 24). Lateness is read from the timing fields alone. A late flight is an infrastructure fault and never a failed procedure, except a call kept late after two flights again, in learning or in evaluation, which is charged to its procedure. A first-round call has no procedure to charge (section 4.1). A decision that meets its deadline has its command sent at the third telemetry stamp after its observation, however long it took. The records time each command at those stamps and hold no time for its arrival at the vehicle, and the same flight flown twice does not always end the same way (section 8.4).

How the delay was set. The rule is the owner's (round four, ruling 8, as ruling 20 restates it). The delay is the smallest whole number of telemetry periods that holds the slowest decision on the platform's own records, counted from its observation, on the build that will fly. A slow decision caused by the run's own work counts. Only a failure that the batch system records against the node or the job does not.

- On the Mac the own lag was at most one step in all 21,029 protocol 3 decisions on record, so two periods would have held there.
- On the cluster, a real-time flight on 13 November had one decision in 53 recorded at 0.201 s, most likely an own lag of two steps. Three periods is the smallest number that holds it. Four periods were not adopted. Three of the five slowest decisions then in the cluster's records had an own lag of four steps, which four periods would not hold either, and their cause was the sandbox's network namespace (round four, rulings 8 and 11).
- After the sandbox change, two periods were tried again on the platform of record. Seven decisions of about 19,800 in the two scripted pilots had an own lag of two steps, so two periods do not hold (ruling 24).
- At three periods no decision in the free checks and pilots had an own lag of three steps. The largest own lags were 0.200 s and 0.201 s, one decision each, so each decision of two steps met its deadline with about 95 ms to spare. Three periods hold, and the delay is 0.3 s (ruling 26, point 1). The rehearsals and the development sample are read by the same rule, and none had a decision of three steps (sections 8.4 and 8.10).

The first flight of a process. Early clock offsets come with the first flight that a process flies. In decisions two to eight they struck 24 of the 46 first flights of a scored-2 hazard phase and none of the 874 later flights. They struck 24 of the 30 first flights of a preflight worker and none of the 268 later ones (`reproduction/timing-diagnostic-split-20261112-091824.json`). Their cause is not known. Such a flight is a step late on three or four of its first eight decisions before any host lag. Under the lateness rule used before the fixed delay (section 8.5), this made whether a phase was ruled late depend in part on what its procedure did. Every hazard phase opens on a close-band flight, and that flight ends after about eight decisions when the procedure enters the zone. The remedy, ruled by the owner on 12 November (9:25 am, item 6) and still open to Simon, is one warm-up flight at the start of every process that flies. That means every phase, every worker of the free check and the late-call check. It stays under the fixed delay and is flown with it (round four, ruling 2). It is a development case flown with the task's reference controller, kept in its own folder and reported, and no gate or analysis reads it. The case is the first calm retention case of the task's development seed book, which lasts well past the first eight decisions with the reference controller and is never a reserved seed. The flight is written to `warmup/` inside the phase's folder (or the worker's), with its timing in `warmup/warmup.json`. It is in no timing row, no count of flights or late flights and no outcome. The gate, the set-aside tool and the analysis do not read it. In the free checks of 16 November at three periods no warm-up decision had an own lag over one step (ruling 26, point 1).

What is recorded and reported. For every flight the records keep four figures read from `decision_lag_s`. They are the median recorded lag, the largest recorded lag, the share of decisions at least 0.1 s late (from a recorded lag of 0.095 s) and the median read on the telemetry step. The records also keep the largest own lag, the number of observations read a step or more late, the number of missed deadlines and the number of commands sent off the deadline. For zone flights they keep the true distance at which the procedure first saw the zone. The first four are read as before, so they compare with scored-2's, and they no longer decide anything (`timing` in `ardupilot/ladder.py`). Apart from the rule above, none of these is part of any gate. The paper reports them by arm (sections 4.9 and 6.5).

### 4.7 The platform

Every flight that is checked or counted flies on one platform: the free checks, the late-call check, the pilots, the development sample and the scored run. The platform is the L40S nodes of the gpu partition of Deakin's cluster, l40s-f-01 to l40s-f-04, each with 64 cores of the Intel Xeon Gold 6448Y. A job uses the processors and asks for no GPU (round four, ruling 7; rulings 22 and 24). One platform flies all of them because a flight's path depends on the simulator build and the host's timing, and the pilot and the sample are worth only what they show about the run that follows them.

How it was chosen. The flights moved from the Mac to the cluster on 13 November. On 16 November the cpu partition was full, and Simon decided that Paper 3 flies on the gpu partition (ruling 22). The L40S nodes are the only node type there whose Python start is close to that of the cpu partition's fast nodes. They became the platform of record by a reading fixed before their flights: two prototype free checks with no late flight, no decision of two steps, every call 'ok' and no reference controller changed. F0 passed on every node (section 8.8).

One node type for a whole run. The four L40S nodes start Python from memory in 14.3 to 15.1 ms, which is what this text means by one speed group, and l40s-f-03 is the slowest. Their kernel releases differ, 5.15.0-176 on l40s-f-01 and 5.15.0-143 on the others, so the release is recorded in every record and reported and is not gated. Instead the sandbox route's tests and the package's containment tests (F0) must pass on every node before any flight there counts (rulings 19 and 22). The simulator is one binary, built once on the cluster from ArduPilot at commit fb77356, whose SHA-256 begins adad8e2377659f74. Every manifest records the binary's hash. Nothing flown on one node type stands for another. If the type of record ever changes, the measurements below are made again. A run flown on any other platform or node type is not a run of this study as written.

How a job flies. A job flies from an archive packed on the Mac from a reviewed commit by `protocol/package_for_cluster.py`. The archive's SHA-256 is printed on the Mac and given on the job's command line, and the job refuses to start unless the archive matches it. The value is not read from inside the archive, because a file inside it could change together with the record that vouches for it. The job unpacks the archive into the node's memory (`/dev/shm`) with a copy of the Python interpreter and the packages it installs there. The run files stay in memory while it flies and are copied to shared storage as section 4.5 says, because the network disks stalled decisions in the smoke runs of 13 November. The cluster clears a job's files in memory when the job ends, so a copy kept there does not outlive the job (ruling 69, point 3). The job runs in bash, not zsh, so that nothing is lowered in priority, and the scheduler and the free check refuse to start at a nice value other than 0. The platform is named in `PAPER3_PLATFORM`, as `cluster` or `mac`, and is never guessed. Each simulator a job flies needs at least 3 cores, one each for the driver, the simulator and the sandboxed child, and a process refuses to start with fewer. One scheduler job flies on a node at a time, because the simulators' ports come from the instance numbers.

What a job asks for. A job asks for 16 cores and 32 GB and flies four simulators at once (ruling 31, point 3). The sources disagreed on the cores: ruling 22 gives 12 for four simulators at three each. Every job that was ever flown flew on 16, the free checks and pilots that the delay and both expectation files were made from among them. So 16 stands, and a job on 12 would be a condition none of those records measured. Twelve is the least the code accepts. The memory request is about seven times the largest peak seen, which was 4.5 GiB for the 16 November rehearsal (job 4800153) with the first 28 minutes of its sample step. `sacct`'s figure counts the files a job keeps in memory as well as its processes (`cluster/README.md`, section of 17 November).

What replaces the quiet Mac. Each job flies on cores the batch system gives it alone, and the deadline rule of section 4.6 is the test of whether the machine kept time. On the cluster there is no load limit, no wait for the load, no hours, no timed pause and no check of the power source. A node's load counts other users' jobs on other cores, so the Mac's load limit does not transfer. Phases are still launched 0.25 s apart. A job's time limit is four days, where the queue allows five (section 8.4 gives the forecast of a job's flying time).

A shared node. Other users' jobs run on the same node, on cores of their own, and the time of a sandboxed call moves by a few per cent with their work. That can change how many decisions have one step of own lag. In the 16 November rehearsal 28% of decisions had one step, against 3% and 6% in the pilots of the afternoon before, on a node where other users' jobs held 10 of 64 processors. It stayed well inside the three-step deadline. How much of that room a procedure written by a model uses is read from the development sample in section 8.4.

What each phase records about the platform. Every manifest records the platform's name, the SHA-256 of the simulator binary and the ArduPilot commit. On the cluster it also records the node, the batch job and its partition, the processor model, the cores the process may use, the one-minute load at its start and the Python version. It also records the process's nice value and the number flying at once. The gate requires on the cluster the node's name, at least 3 allowed cores and a nice value of 0 with no load limit applied (`check_platform` in `protocol/validate3.py`). It does not check the simulator binary. The frozen values (section 9.2) name the partition, node type and processor model, and the gate compares a scored run with them. The binary's hash is recorded in every manifest and is compared by the people who read the manifests, not by the gate.

The Mac is not a fallback. It flew this protocol's development work until 13 November, and round four's ruling 7 kept it as the fallback if the free check failed on the cluster for a reason that could not be put right. Ruling 83 ends that. The frozen platform is the cluster's L40S nodes, and the gate refuses a scored run whose records name another, so a run on the Mac would need a new freeze. If the cluster fails for a reason that cannot be put right, the run waits and Simon decides. The code keeps the Mac's rules, none of which applies on the cluster. They are a one-minute load limit of 4.89 checked before every counted flight, and hours of 6 pm to 7 am [PICKED 10] with a scheduler pause at 4:30 am. The Mac also had to be on mains power with the lid open, with nobody using it, and with no software update or indexing of the run folder. The limit was set from a timing preflight of 11 and 12 November (section 8.5).

What was measured on the L40S nodes. The Python start, the start of the sandbox's namespaces, the keyring fix and that the simulator binary runs (ruling 22). F0 on every node (section 8.8). The free checks, scripted pilots and rehearsals of sections 8.9 and 8.10, which give every decision's own lag and every sandboxed call's status and wall time. The clock speed and governor, read from records already made: each of the 13 jobs flown on the four nodes on 16 November printed a median of 2100 MHz and no frequency governor at its start (ruling 31, point 4). A sandboxed call's wall time on the new wheel. The bench `cluster/bench_cluster.py` was not run, because every flight on the wheel times each of its calls with the route, and those are the calls the delay has to hold (section 8.4 gives them). A job's memory peak, from `sacct`.

The reference controllers ignore the zone, so the hazard flights of a free check end when the drone enters it. That does not affect lag, which does not depend on what the command is, and whether those flights met their goals is evidence of nothing.

### 4.8 The sandbox route and the Paper 3 wheel

Every decision runs the procedure and the guard check as two sandboxed calls, each a new process, allowed 2 s of processor time and 5 s of wall time (round three, ruling 1). On Linux the second protocol's wheel made a network namespace, the kernel's separate copy of the network, for every call and destroyed it afterwards. On the cluster on 13 November that sometimes stalled a call. Three calls took 262 to 367 ms, each waiting 217 to 321 ms with nothing computing. One made its decision four steps late, which no delay up to four periods holds (round four, ruling 11, and 'What the cluster showed this morning', point 3). A probe of the sandbox's start traced the stall to the network namespace: without it, the largest start among four callers took 5.3 ms. The likely mechanism, not shown, is that the kernel tears these namespaces down in batches under a lock the whole node shares (point 4). Fewer simulators at once did not remove the stall (ruling 16).

The route. The Paper 3 wheel gives each driver process one network namespace. A holder process, `unshare --user --map-root-user --net cat`, makes it once, and every call the driver makes joins it through `nsenter --target H --user --net --preserve-credentials` in front of the call's own command. Each call still makes its own user, process-ID, mount, inter-process communication and host-name namespaces inside the holder's, and runs in the same empty read-only root with the same drop of privileges and the same limits as before. It still has no network. The shared namespace has only a loopback, which is down, and no call can bring it up (ruling 17). Each driver process starts its own holder before anything in it makes a call, and makes one trusted call through it, so a node where the route cannot run refuses before any flight. No two processes share a holder, and a holder ends when its process ends (ruling 17). The new wheel has no command that makes a network namespace for each call, so it cannot fall back to the old route, and which route a run is on is set by which wheel is installed (ruling 17). The route is named `netns-per-process+keyring-per-call/2`. On macOS the sandbox is `sandbox-exec`, unchanged.

The session keyring. On Linux every call used to inherit the driver's session keyring, a store of small secrets the kernel keeps for a login session. In a batch job on the cluster that keyring belongs to the batch system's own service and is shared by every job on the node. A probe found that without a fix a call could find, read and link a key in it and add one (round four, ruling 15; ruling 18; job 4799098). A key one call left was found by the next. In the new wheel each call joins a new, empty session keyring before its program starts, and the call's first lines check that it is empty. If the join fails, the call is not made and the flight stops (ruling 18). Each call keeps a user namespace of its own, because the kernel keeps a user keyring for each user namespace, which the session keyring does not cover (ruling 18).

The filter. The call's first lines then install a seccomp filter, a short program the kernel runs on every system call the process makes. It refuses the three key system calls (`add_key`, `request_key` and `keyctl`), so that a call cannot even see that a key of the same user exists by guessing its number. It refuses `socket`, `socketpair` and `io_uring_setup`, so that a call can create nothing in the network namespace it shares with later calls. And it kills a process that makes a system call as another machine type (rulings 18 and 23). The socket part was added after the review of the first build found that a call could pass 16 bits to the next through IPv6 flow labels. Flow labels outlive a call, are not sockets and were in no table the route counted. That first build, `+paper3.1`, is never flown of record (ruling 23). On the rebuilt wheel the reviewer's probe was refused at `socket()` and passed nothing (ruling 23; jobs 4799229 and 4799230). No procedure needs a key or a socket, and the source check refuses any import other than `math` before the sandbox is reached (ruling 18). The filter goes beyond what Simon was told on 13 November, and it is on his list (Open to Simon).

When a holder is spent, replaced or dead. The parent stops a call at the wall-clock limit, for a reply too large or breaking the channel's framing, or after an exception in the parent during the call. A stopped call marks the holder spent, because something it did may outlive the kill. The next call ends a spent holder and starts a new one. After every call that ended normally the route also counts the lines of the holder's socket tables and of its IPv6 flow-label table, and any change from the counts read when the holder was ready marks it spent. Each replacement is recorded with the call's number, its status and its reason: 'stopped', or 'residue' with the table named (rulings 17 and 23; Build A, A2). Before and after each call the route checks that the holder is alive, still in the network namespace recorded when it started, and owned by this process. If it is not, the call raises `RouteError`, nothing catches it, and the flight stops. A dead holder that is not spent is never replaced, since that would hide a fault (ruling 17). Scripted and reference procedures are never stopped and leave nothing behind, so the free check and the pass script fail any run in which a holder was replaced (Build B, B2).

What each phase records, and what the gate asks. Every phase's manifest records the route under `sandbox`: its name, the holder's process number, its user and network namespaces, and the driver's process number. It also records the kernel release, the machine type, whether keyrings were joined, how the route started and the counts of the socket tables. At its end each phase writes `sandbox-end.json` with the number of calls and every replacement (Build A, A2; Build B, B1). The free check and the late-call check record the same. The gate refuses a phase whose manifest names no route, or a route other than its platform's: `netns-per-process+keyring-per-call/2` on the cluster and `sandbox-exec` on the Mac, written out in `EXPECTED_ROUTE` in `ardupilot/scored.py` so that an old wheel cannot meet them. It refuses a cluster phase that names no kernel release, that has no `sandbox-end.json`, or that replaced a holder for a reason other than 'stopped' or 'residue' or because the holder died. It refuses a run root whose phases name more than one route, and it lists the kernel releases without gating them (`check_route` in `protocol/validate3.py`). A phase that finds a replacement the gate would refuse stops at once, so the chain does not fly on, and the set-aside tool refuses to set such a phase aside (ruling 25). A run therefore carries four marks of its wheel: the wheel's hash in the core check, the installed version, the route and the source fingerprint (ruling 19).

Where the cost now falls. A phase that ends lets its holder go, which destroys a network namespace, and a phase that starts makes one. While the kernel does either, a call of another phase on the same node that is joining its own namespace waits, in the pilots at two periods for 50 to 125 ms. Sixteen of the 17 calls over 85 ms in those pilots came 1 to 3 s after one phase ended and the next started on the node (ruling 24). Three periods take a wait of this size: a decision of about 100 ms needs about 195 ms more to miss its deadline, and the largest call seen then was 165.4 ms. Two phase boundaries falling together could make a longer wait, and a flight late for that reason is handled like any late flight. A remedy at the source, starting and ending holders only while nothing flies on the node, would need the scheduler to fly its phases in waves, and it is not built (ruling 24).

Containment was inspected again at each change of the fingerprint. Codex's leak harness found no path by which a held-out flight, another arm's flights or procedures, or anything in the route's records reaches a request or a token-count body. At the commit where the fingerprint became cf270a5d, every one of the 105 saved request payloads matched the reference byte for byte (ruling 56, point 8; rulings 45, 47 and 50 for the earlier commits). The run's file of message ids was opened only by the provider's own claim function, and all four planted faults were caught. The inspection is run again at each change of the fingerprint, as section 2.2 says (ruling 26, point 5; ruling 89, point 3). Build N changes the seed book and not `ladder.py` or the wheel, so for it the inspection is the part that needs no model call. The saved request payloads are rebuilt from build N's code and compared byte for byte with the reference, as in ruling 56, point 8, and Codex ran the leak harness on build N's changes with no model call; both clean runs matched all 108 retained files (ruling 96, point 1). The reviewer of build N ran the payload part at a22d5c7e: in two clean runs all 105 payloads matched, and none of the 72 new seed numbers was found in a request (ruling 93, point 1). The harness stands in for the simulator and the sandbox, so it does not test the operating system's containment, which the route's own tests cover. What the wheel's second review left open lies outside the network namespace and is the same in the old wheel (ruling 23). It is timing through the node's page cache or processor, which needs `open` and a clock, both refused by the source check.

### 4.9 What the paper says about timing, the platform and the sandbox

The paper or its supplement must say these things (round four, ruling 6, with the additions named). The manuscript plan places each item in the article or in the supplement (`manuscript/PLAN-4.md`, section 10).

- Every command in every flight of this protocol was applied three telemetry periods, 0.3 s of simulated time, after the observation it was computed from, in every arm, task and phase.
- Why. On the Mac that flew the earlier work, a decision's two sandboxed calls took 74 to 76 ms at best and 94 to 100 ms in a slow group, against about 80 ms that fitted inside one period. The Mac was a managed machine whose security software inspects every program start and could not be changed. On the cluster, before the sandbox change, the calls took 128 to 133 ms at the median in a real-time flight and 183 ms at the worst, so a decision there could take two periods.
- The cause of the Mac's slow group was not found.
- Scored-2 sent each command as soon as it was decided, so the two protocols' flights differ in this, and any comparison across them says so.
- The deadline was counted from the observation's own telemetry stamp. From decision 75 of a flight the observation itself was read a step after its schedule in about half the decisions, in both protocols, and the paper gives the likely cause.
- At the 5 m/s speed limit, 0.3 s is up to 1.5 m more on the previous command, and this mattered. The two scripted sources, procedures the model wrote in scored-2 for commands that took effect at once, passed 1.1 to 2.7 m from a zone's edge without the delay. With it, 1 of their 18 threat flights ended differently at 0.2 s, 6 and then 7 at 0.3 s and 10 at 0.4 s. Most of these entered the zone and a few landed hard, while every reference flight ended as before.
- Timing by arm: the counts of missed deadlines and of commands sent at other than the third telemetry stamp. With them go the number of calls flown again and of flights kept late, and how they were handled (round two, ruling 3; ruling 74, point 5). A missed deadline or an off-stamp command makes a flight late (`ardupilot/ladder.py`, line 157), and a late flight is flown again, so in the flights that count both can be above zero only in a flight kept late. The analysis counts missed deadlines by arm. The commands sent off the third stamp are counted by arm from the kept-late flights' `off_deadline_commands`, grouped by arm as the analysis groups flights, by a short descriptive script outside the fingerprint. If no flight is kept late, both counts are zero and nothing is computed (ruling 80, point 3).
- The three sensitivity analyses of section 6.2, for kept-late evaluation flights, for learning calls kept late and for learning blocks flown again after the choice arose. For each, the three primary intervals and the six D − A intervals under both fills, reported apart, with any name that changes, or that its list was empty. None of them changes the recorded estimates (rulings 39, 49, 78, 81 and 87).
- The sample-size planning was first fitted to scored-2's adoption rates, from flights without the delay, and its rescue rates came from a repair that showed the model no procedure's flights. The paper says what the second sample showed against it and how the simulation was corrected (sections 6.6 and 8.4).
- The six per-task D − A intervals were moved from primary to secondary before any scored outcome existed, because the corrected simulation showed that 40 blocks of one task cannot name a gain smaller than the margin. The plan fixed before the development sample did not allow this change, so the paper reports it as a departure, gives that reason and reports the six in full (sections 6.6 and 8.4; ruling 88, point 2).
- Which platform flew which flights. The sandbox around each procedure differs by platform, namespaces and a changed root directory on Linux and `sandbox-exec` on macOS, so a call's waiting time is not comparable across them.
- From the sandbox change, every Linux flight of this protocol ran each sandboxed call in a user, process-ID, mount, inter-process communication and host-name namespace of its own. Each driver process had one network namespace with no network, each call a new empty session keyring, and the key system calls and the creation of sockets were refused. The paper says why, that a stopped call's namespace was never joined again, and that the first build of the wheel let a call pass a few bits to the next through IPv6 flow labels. Its review found that before any procedure written by a model had run in it (ruling 17).
- Where the network namespace's cost now falls, at the boundaries between phases (ruling 24).
- Every request names the flights it mentions by alias, in all four arms, as scored-2's requests did, so the alias rule does not change what arm C is sent compared with scored-2 (ruling 71, point 4).
- The learner's requests carry the procedure-only sentence and the delay sentence, which scored-2's did not, and every request for a reply carried the field that tells the gateway not to use its store (section 2.2).
- The gateway returned stored replies as new ones in the first sample, how that was found and how it was handled (sections 7.1 and 8.11).
- The earlier comparison changed two things together, whether the adoption test flew the zone and whether the flights shown had the zone in them, and its repair showed no procedure's flights. The four arms here separate those two and add a choice the earlier comparison held fixed, repair against a fresh draw (ruling 71, point 3).
- In the supplement, the position test's results and what it measures, as a diagnostic of readiness (section 8.4; ruling 74, point 5).
- The spend as the gateway charged it, and the count of blocks lost with a dead job and of blocks set aside, by kind of fault (sections 4.5 and 7.1).

### 4.10 ArduPilot's own planner, an exploratory check

ArduPilot Copter has its own tool for flying around a stay-out area. It is a path planner, chosen by the parameter `OA_TYPE`, that steers toward a waypoint around exclusion fences. A reader will ask how this tool does on the same zone flights, and on 20 November Simon said yes to flying it (ruling 97, point 2). The check flies the planner on the 72 evaluation scenarios the arms are evaluated on, with scripted flights and no model call, and gives its rates of success and of zone entry by task and distance band. It is descriptive. It makes no test against any arm and is reported apart from the scored comparisons, and nothing in it can move a scored interval.

Its design is fixed in `notes/PLANNER-CHECK-DESIGN-20261120.md` at commit 57f302b8, which the freeze merges. The design was first fixed at 3b0970bb (ruling 98, point 1). The later commits correct its account of ArduPilot after two reviews, one of them Codex's against the firmware source, and add the shake-down's record; none changes a setting, a parameter or a rule. It was written before any scored result existed. The settings, what the planner is told and when, the flights, the scoring, the faults, the records and the build are all in that note, and this section does not repeat them. Seven settings fly: the planner off, and six settings of the two planners, Dijkstra and BendyRuler. The headline is setting 1, Dijkstra with its default planning margin. Some flight settings differ from ArduPilot's defaults because the planner cannot act without them, among them `GUID_OPTIONS` bit 6 and the fence switched on. It is named before any flight so that the discussion cannot pick the best setting afterwards, and the paper reports all seven.

The evaluation seeds are scored seeds, so the check flies only after the freeze, on a node the scored run leaves free. It writes to its own run root, its records are kept apart from the scored run's, and the scored run's gate and analysis never read them. Its code is in `planner_check/`, outside the source fingerprint, which stays 8700e012… over 86 files (ruling 98, point 4). This text names the design's commit, and the freeze merges the branch that holds it, so the design and the check's code are in the frozen history (section 9.2; ruling 98, point 3).

Two facts about ArduPilot's avoidance, read in the version the study flies, bear on how the study's own flights are described (ruling 98, point 2, which corrects ruling 92, point 1). The path planner works toward waypoints in Auto and RTL, and toward a Guided position target only when bit 6 of `GUID_OPTIONS` is set. That parameter's default is 0. The path planner ignores velocity commands, which is how the study's procedures fly. ArduPilot's simpler fence avoidance (`AVOID_ENABLE`, on by default) would act on those velocity commands, slowing or stopping them short of the zone, if the zone were loaded as an enabled fence. The study flies with the fence off (`FENCE_ENABLE` 0) and the planner off (`OA_TYPE` 0), both ArduPilot's defaults, so neither acts on any of its flights.

## 6. Analysis

The analysis script (`protocol/analysis3.py`) and the gate (`protocol/validate3.py`) are frozen with this protocol. The analysis runs once, on a run the gate has passed. It writes a record of its run into each run root, after the gate passes and before any outcome is read, and refuses to run again. So a crash after that point uses up the run. If that happens, the crash and the record, with the hashes of the analysis sources, go to Simon, and a second run needs a ruling that names what changed in the code and why (rulings 39 and 46, point 7). In a scored run the analysis refuses a resample count or seed other than the protocol's, and a manifest whose pilot flag does not match the mode (ruling 49, point 3).

### 6.1 The outcome

For each task the analysis builds one table of outcomes with a cell for every arm, block and evaluation scenario: 4 arms by 40 blocks by 24 scenarios. A cell is 1 if the arm adopted a source and that source met the goal on the scenario, and 0 otherwise. An arm that adopted nothing has 0 in all 24 cells of its block. A block whose c0 was not a valid procedure counts as 0 in every cell of c0's own success, and the number of such blocks is printed. The scenarios are the planned ones, taken from the seeds file, so nothing drops out when an arm fails to adopt.

### 6.2 Intervals

A contrast between two arms is the difference of their means over the cells in question. Its interval comes from a crossed bootstrap with 10,000 resamples and seed 20261112. Each resample draws blocks with replacement, keeping a block's four arms together. It also draws scenarios with replacement within each band, and the same scenario draw applies to every block and arm. The bands keep equal weight [PICKED 8]. Intervals are percentile intervals. Each is a pointwise interval for its own contrast. The three primary intervals together make no claim of joint coverage, and neither do the secondary ones.

Each task's draws come from a generator of its own, seeded with `[BOOT_SEED, 0, the task's index]`, which nothing else uses; the index is 0 for reach, 1 for reach3d and 2 for land (`TASKS` in `protocol/contract3.py`). The draws are made in this order: all bands, each band, then the middle and wide bands together. The pooled steps use each task's all-band draws. A change to a secondary estimate therefore cannot move a primary interval (ruling 46, point 1).

Late flights in evaluation. An evaluation phase that was flown again (section 4.6) can leave some flights that were kept late. The analysis counts them by arm. It repeats the three primary intervals and the six per-task D − A intervals twice. Once those flights' cells are filled in the way that is worst for each contrast, and once in the way that is best, so both bounds are on the page. The primary and the secondary intervals are reported apart. For the lower bound of X − Y, each kept-late cell in X is 0 and each in Y is 1, and the upper bound is the reverse. A flight both arms share has the same cell in both and cancels. The six D − A intervals share one pair of fills, and each pooled step has its own. The names of section 6.4 are shown for each fill. Keeping the first attempt that was on time selects attempts by their timing. So for every phase that was flown again, which is any evaluation phase with a set-aside attempt whatever the reason, the analysis also reports each attempt's reason. It reports too how many cells of each set-aside attempt differ from the attempt kept (ruling 39, point 2; ruling 49, point 4).

Calls kept late in learning. A learning call that was set aside and replaced by an on-time attempt never enters learning, so it is counted by arm as a call flown again and nothing more. A call kept late on its third attempt stays in the block. If its result decided whether a candidate was adopted, or its flights were shown to the model when it wrote a later candidate, it can touch every evaluation outcome of that arm in that block. This text calls one arm's 24 evaluation cells in one block an arm-block. The rule below is fixed by ruling 78, point 2.

- The list. Every arm-block such a call touched is listed by task, block, arm and call. A trial kept late touches each arm that decided on its candidate by that trial, so the trial of c0 that arms B and C share touches both. A first-round call kept late touches each arm whose first request showed its flights: arms A, B and C for the matched list (P0), whose request writes c0 and the fresh candidates, and arm D for the threat list (P1). The list is made from the learning timing rows the analysis already reads (`calls_flown_again` and `arms_of_call` in `protocol/analysis3.py`) and from each block's `arms.json` and requests. If a call kept late cannot be tied to its arms, the list is reported as incomplete, with that call named, and is never called empty (ruling 87, point 4). If the list is complete and empty, the paper says so and nothing more is computed.
- The two fills. Otherwise each of the three primary contrasts and the six per-task D − A contrasts is computed twice more. For X − Y, the upper fill sets every cell of a listed arm-block of X to 1 and of Y to 0, whether or not that arm adopted, and the lower fill does the reverse. Each listed arm-block is filled on its own, even where X and Y adopted the same source in that block. A listed block is one whose learning might have gone otherwise, and had it, two arms that adopted the same source might have adopted different ones. A re-flown block whose c0 passed, where A, B and C all adopted c0, is the case the second list below exists to bound, so cancelling such arms would hide the very bias it measures. This corrects ruling 80, point 4, which cancelled them, after the cold review by Fable found the flaw; `protocol/sensitivity3.py` passes every source to `filled` as unknown so that nothing cancels (aa9a71c1 on `claude/paper3-n40-expect`). The cancel stays only for a kept-late evaluation flight of section 7.2, which is one flight that both arms flew (`filled` in `analysis3.py`). Every other cell keeps its recorded value, kept-late evaluation cells included, and no denominator changes.
- The intervals. Each fill uses the resamples of the recorded intervals, with the same seed and the same order of draws. The six D − A intervals share one pair of fills, and each pooled step has its own.
- What is reported. The paper gives each fill's point estimate and interval beside the recorded result, the three primary intervals and the six secondary ones each reported apart, and marks every contrast whose name under section 6.4 would change under either fill. This is a sensitivity analysis. The recorded estimates are unchanged.

The computation is `protocol/sensitivity3.py`, a separate script outside the source fingerprint, written to this rule and reproduced by Codex before the paper states it (ruling 78, point 3). The freeze fixes it by its hash in the archive's `SOURCE.json`, and the sealing ruling records that hash. The development sample had no call kept late.

Blocks flown again after the choice arose. A learning block set aside for a fault of section 7.1, or lost with a dead job, is flown again under the same name and seeds, so the model draws its first candidates again. A block whose c0 failed its zone trial goes on to fresh draws and a repair, so it makes more calls and flies more trials than a block whose c0 passed, and is more likely to meet a fault or a job's end. Flying it again can replace it with a block whose c0 passed, where arms B and C adopt the same procedure. That would pull B − A and C − B towards zero, and D − C through C. The rule below is fixed by rulings 81 and 87.

- The list. A learning block is listed if it was set aside for a fault or lost with a dead job, and an attempt set aside had already sent a request for a fresh candidate or a repair, whatever came back. Such a request is sent only after a trial failed, so it marks an attempt in which the choice had arisen. Calls flown again for lateness do not draw the block again and are not counted.
- What counts as sent. The kind of each request is read from the set-aside attempt's own call records, its usage and provider rows. The driver writes a slot's request file before it calls the model, and writes those rows only after the call returns. A job killed during a call can therefore leave a request file with no row. A fresh or repair slot that holds a request file and has no row counts as sent, and is marked 'sending not established'. Counting an unknown as sent can only widen the range the fills give, so it cannot hide an effect. Of the request files only the folder names are read, which shows which slots hold one. Their contents are never read, because a request shows earlier flights and no outcome of a set-aside attempt is read. If an attempt's candidate folders were not kept, so that what was due next cannot be read, and no row shows such a request, the attempt is listed for all four arms of its block and marked 'not determinable'.
- What is reported. The paper gives three counts, each named by the unit it counts: the set-aside learning attempts, by reason; the distinct blocks with any set-aside attempt; and the distinct listed blocks, with their attempts counted as sent, sending not established or not determinable. If an attempt cannot be placed in a block, the list is reported as incomplete, with what was not placed, and is never called empty. If the list is complete and empty, the paper says that no set-aside attempt has a recorded fresh or repair request, and nothing more is computed.
- The fills. Otherwise all four arm-blocks of each listed block's kept attempt are treated as the arm-blocks of the rule above. The three primary contrasts and the six per-task D − A contrasts are filled both ways by that rule and reported apart, beside the recorded result. This is a sensitivity analysis, and the recorded estimates are unchanged.

`protocol/sensitivity3.py` computes it, and Codex reproduces it (ruling 81, point 4; ruling 87). Codex also reproduces the three primary intervals and RQ4's six D − A intervals from the scored records with its own code, before the paper states them, and the paper reports whether every name matched. The development sample had no block set aside.

### 6.3 The three primary intervals, and the six that answer RQ4

The primary intervals are B − A, C − B and D − C, each over all 24 scenarios and pooled over the three tasks with equal weight. The tasks are resampled independently. C − B is the central contrast and is reported first. Each pooled step is printed beside its three per-task estimates and intervals, which are secondary. The number of blocks for a pooled step is the blocks of the three tasks added together, 3 × 40 = 120, since the step takes their cells together (ruling 46, point 2).

RQ4 is answered by six secondary intervals:

- for each of the three tasks, D − A on the 8 close scenarios;
- for each of the three tasks, D − A on the 16 middle and wide scenarios together.

They are computed exactly as they were when they were primary, from the same draws, and named by section 6.4's table. The paper reports all six in full as RQ4's answer, whatever their names (ruling 88, point 2). At 40 blocks five of the six miss the planning target, 80% of runs resolved, in some of the simulation's 324 settings. In the worst setting they resolve in only 19.1% to 27.8% of simulated runs, while D − A on close targets in reach resolves in 99.9% (section 6.6). So the study cannot promise to resolve them. That is not a forecast that most will be unresolved. Only 61 of the 324 settings have four or more of the six each more likely unresolved than resolved, 139 have none, and the settings carry no probabilities (ruling 96, point 2). An unresolved interval means that 40 blocks of one task could not settle the question. It is not evidence that arms D and A do not differ.

What each can be read as, and what it cannot.

- D − A, close targets. The difference, for one task, between learning with the zone shown, tested and repaired, and learning by test and selection with no zone, on fresh flights with the target 3 to 4 m past the zone. It includes failures to adopt. It cannot be read as the effect of any one part, as the effect of telling the model about zones, since every arm is told, or as success given adoption. It is also not a repeat of scored-2's comparison: arm A samples afresh where scored-2's arm repaired, and every candidate here must pass on all three bands.
- D − A, distant targets. The same total with the target 9 to 21 m past the zone. A difference from the close band shows that the total depends on this family of geometry. It cannot be put down to clearance alone (section 3).
- B − A. What changes when the adoption test includes the zone and nothing else does. It can be negative, because a stricter test adopts less often. It cannot be read as the value of testing in a system that also repairs.
- C − B. What revision from the failed zone flights adds over sampling afresh without them, from the same first candidate, under the same test and the same number of slots. It is taken over all blocks, including those in which c0 passed its threat trial and arms B and C were the same arm. It cannot be read as the effect of revision alone or of feedback alone (section 1.2). Where arm B adopts nothing in a task, that task's C − B is arm C's own success rate.
- D − C. What seeing the zone before the first candidate adds when the zone test and repair are already present. It cannot be read as the effect of zone examples without testing, which is not an arm.
- Every pooled step is an average over these three tasks. It says nothing about other tasks.

RQ4 asks whether the total gain holds when the target lies well clear of the zone. Its answer is the name of each task's D − A interval on the distant targets, read beside the one on close targets. The paper does not say from the two names that the gain differs between close and distant targets. For that it reports, for each task, the interval of D − A on close targets minus D − A on distant targets, made from the same resamples. That difference is secondary and descriptive. The sample-size work did not plan its resolution, and it is not given one of the names (ruling 38, point 4). "The same resamples" means that the close and distant parts of this difference share one draw; that draw has its own generator, `[20261112, 2, task]`, and is not the draw of the named intervals, `[20261112, 0, task]`. The conditional C − B of section 6.5 uses a third, `[20261112, 1, task]`. RQ4's reported intervals are always the named ones, under `secondary_intervals` in `analysis.json`, never the two parts printed inside the close-minus-distant record.

How each task's pair of names answers RQ4:

Here "a gain" means either gain name of section 6.4, a gain or a gain smaller than the margin.

| On close targets | On distant targets | The paper says |
|---|---|---|
| a gain | a gain | the total gain held on distant targets for this task |
| a gain | no gain or loss as large as the margin | a gain on close targets; on distant targets no gain or loss as large as the margin |
| a gain | unresolved | a gain on close targets; on distant targets the study cannot say |
| no gain or loss as large as the margin, or unresolved | a gain, no gain or loss as large as the margin, or unresolved | each name as it stands, with no statement about the gain holding |
| a loss on either band | anything | each name as it stands, and the loss named with its band |
| either band has one of the names for nothing to compare | anything | that name, and the other as it stands |

None of these rows says that the gain differs between close and distant targets; only the interval of the difference speaks to that.

### 6.4 What each result is called

Each primary interval, and each of RQ4's six D − A intervals, is given one name, fixed here. The names combine a test of difference from 0, read from the 95% interval, with a test of equivalence within the margin, read from the 90% interval. Lakens (2017) sets out the 4 combinations and uses a 90% interval for the equivalence test because it is two one-sided tests at the 0.05 level. The intervals here are bootstrap intervals and not that article's t tests. The margin is 0.20 [PICKED 1].

| The 95% interval | The 90% interval | The result is called | Key in `analysis.json` |
|---|---|---|---|
| lies wholly above 0, or wholly below | does not lie wholly inside −0.20 to +0.20 | a gain, or a loss | `different_not_equivalent` |
| lies wholly above 0, or wholly below | lies wholly inside −0.20 to +0.20 | a gain, or a loss, smaller than the margin | `different_and_equivalent` |
| includes 0 | lies wholly inside −0.20 to +0.20 | no gain or loss as large as the margin | `equivalent_not_different` |
| includes 0 | does not lie wholly inside −0.20 to +0.20 | unresolved (Lakens's 'undetermined') | `unresolved` |

A gain whose 95% interval lies wholly above +0.20 is also called a gain larger than the margin, and a loss wholly below −0.20 a loss larger than the margin (the keys in `analysis.json` are `useful_gain` and `useful_loss`). The names at a margin of 0.20 are the answers. The same table is reported with a margin of 0.10 beside them and never replaces them. The margin is a planning assumption. This protocol makes no claim about what a gain of a given size is worth.

Every comparison is strict. A 95% interval whose end is exactly 0 includes 0. A 90% interval whose end is exactly −0.20 or +0.20 does not lie wholly inside the margin, and a 95% interval whose lower end is exactly +0.20 is not a gain larger than the margin (`classify` in `protocol/analysis3.py`). With success shares on 40 blocks such ties can happen.

'No gain or loss as large as the margin' supports the absence of an effect that large. It does not show that there is no effect. An unresolved interval is reported as unresolved.

Three more names apply when the two arms have nothing to compare. The rule is made on the cells that enter the contrast and not on the interval's width, because an interval can have no width and still show a difference: one arm succeeding everywhere and the other nowhere gives exactly 1. For the cells of the two arms compared:

| The cells | The result is called | Key in `analysis.json` |
|---|---|---|
| both arms 0 in every cell | no success in either arm | `no_success_in_either_arm` |
| both arms 1 in every cell | every flight succeeded in both arms | `every_flight_succeeded_in_both_arms` |
| otherwise, the two arms equal cell for cell | identical outcomes in all N blocks, with N | `identical_outcomes` |

Each of these replaces the names above for that interval, in `analysis.json` and in the paper. None of them is 'no gain or loss as large as the margin': the bootstrap has nothing to resample, so it supports no statement about the margin. For 'identical outcomes' the paper gives the one-sided 95% bound on the share of blocks in which the two arms would differ, 1 − 0.05^(1/N), with N the blocks of the contrast. That is 7.2% for one task's 40 blocks. A pooled step takes N as the blocks of the three tasks together, 120, which gives 2.5%, and each task's own result is named the same way beside it. A step with one block reads 'identical outcomes in the 1 block'. Where a task has no D − A to compare, its record carries `nothing_to_compare` and no classification (ruling 38, point 2; ruling 46, points 2 and 3).

One further name applies to the pooled steps only. When one task's 95% interval for a step lies wholly above 0 and another's lies wholly below, the step is called task-dependent and its pooled name is not used (SIZE:71). Task-dependent is a result. It says that the step raised success on one task and lowered it on another, each shown by that task's own interval.

How the pooled name and the per-task estimates are read together. One table fixes the answer, the same for each pooled step. The signs are those of the three per-task point estimates, and an estimate of exactly 0 has no sign and differs from neither (ruling 38, point 3).

| The pooled result | The per-task signs do not differ | The per-task signs differ |
|---|---|---|
| task-dependent | cannot occur; if it did, the analysis would stop with an error and the paper would give the step's per-task intervals with no pooled name | the answer: the step raised success on the tasks named and lowered it on the tasks named |
| a gain or a loss, of either size | the answer: a pooled gain, or loss, over these three tasks | the same answer, and the task whose estimate has the other sign is named beside it with its interval |
| no pooled gain or loss as large as the margin | the answer: no pooled gain or loss that large over these three tasks | not settled. The paper says the average is near zero while the tasks' estimates point in opposite directions, gives each with its interval, and does not say that the step made no difference |
| unresolved | unresolved | unresolved, with the signs stated |
| one of the three names for nothing to compare | that name | cannot occur |

The pooled B − A is the case where this matters most. The planning simulation of 11 November expected its three task values to have opposite signs, because there arm B adopted almost nothing on reach and did adopt on the other two tasks. At 32 blocks it named the step task-dependent in about 51% of simulated runs when distant targets are no easier than close ones. In 17% to 19% of runs in the middle case the rule stayed silent and the step was named 'no gain or loss as large as the margin' (`notes/SAMPLE-SIZE-RERUN-20261111.md`, Tables 2 and 3). The corrected simulation has arm B adopt rarely on every task. At 40 blocks the three task means of B − A are all negative in 292 of its 324 combinations. In the other 32 they differ in sign, and there the step is named task-dependent in 2.4% to 23.9% of runs and 'no gain or loss as large as the margin' in up to 91.9% (`reproduction/ladder-pooled3-20261119b.json`). These are planning inputs and not predictions the study must confirm. The table above is the answer to that weakness: it does not let the pooled name stand alone when the tasks disagree. A rule on the difference between two tasks was tried in the 11 November simulation. It fired in up to 33.5% of C − B runs and 36.1% of D − C runs in which the tasks agree in sign. It would have taken the pooled name away from the central contrast in about one run in three, so the present rule stays.

Pooled B − A is a primary interval. The owner once proposed making it secondary, because a pooled mean of effects that may have opposite signs answers no question a designer has. It was kept, because the table above already names that result and prints each task's own interval beside it (ruling 34), and Simon's choice of the three primary intervals keeps it (ruling 88).

### 6.5 Secondary estimates

Named in advance (DESIGN:50–55). Each is computed by `analysis3.py`, except the last two. The second to last is worked by addition and division from fields `analysis3.py` writes, and the last is read from the run's records by `protocol/run_records3.py`. This list names nothing that those scripts do not compute or write (ruling 38, point 5). They carry the same intervals where they are contrasts.

- RQ4's six per-task D − A intervals, close and distant for each task, named by section 6.4's table and reported in full (section 6.3; ruling 88, point 2).
- Every contrast in each band, and every step for each task. Each is named by section 6.4's table in `analysis.json`, and those names are secondary results.
- C − B within the blocks where c0 was a valid procedure and failed its threat trial, since that failure comes before the two arms part. Blocks where c0 was not valid are counted and left out.
- Model calls, tokens and list cost, given twice: the unique spend of the experiment, and each arm's cost as if it had run alone, charged in full for any request or trial it shares. The spend of blocks that were set aside is given beside the unique spend, with calls of unknown cost counted apart.
- The candidate at which each arm adopted, and how often each arm adopted nothing.
- By kind of candidate (c0, c1, fresh, repair), how many slots were valid at the first call, valid after the format repair and not valid. And the number of message ids that appear more than once, which the gate requires to be 0.
- How flights ended, by arm.
- The success of c0 on the evaluation scenarios.
- Retention on the ordinary scenarios, by arm.
- The share of fresh candidates whose source is identical to their block's c0.
- By arm, the timing figures of section 4.6, for learning trial flights, zone evaluation flights and retention flights. They are the median and largest recorded lag, the share of decisions at least 0.1 s late, the largest own lag, the observations read a step or more late and the missed deadlines. They also include the first-sight distance and the procedure's and the guard's call times. With them come the calls flown again and the flights kept late. The lag figures and the counts of late observations and missed deadlines are read by `ladder.timing` from each flight's own steps in its `outcome.json`. The call times come from the flight's protected `episode.log`, and the gate passes them on without checking them, which is acceptable because they are descriptive. The calls flown again and the flights kept late come from each learning block's `timing.json`, and a first-round call counts for arms A, B and C (P0) or arm D (P1). A flight two arms share counts in both arms' figures. The share of decisions at least 0.1 s late is measured from each decision's scheduled observation to the moment it was ready, so it includes any clock offset of the observation and each flight's first decision. It does not measure when a command was sent. First-sight distance is computed again inside `analysis3.py`, since the original reads a folder, and a review compares the two (round two, ruling 3; round four, ruling 6; ruling 46, point 5). The count of commands sent off the third stamp is not in this table. It is read from the kept-late flights as section 4.9 says (ruling 80, point 3).
- For each task and arm, the count of blocks with a success, the success among blocks that adopted and the mean success in each distance band. All are descriptive and have no interval (ruling 55, point 3).

### 6.6 Sample size

N is 40 blocks per task. Simon chose it on 19 November from the corrected planning simulation, before any scored outcome existed (ruling 88). It is not changed after any scored outcome exists, and neither are the margin or the set of primary intervals (SCHEMA:44). The run is never extended.

What the simulation does. It plays the whole study many times on made-up blocks. In each simulated block it draws which arms adopt and, for each adopted procedure, a set of evaluation outcomes taken from scored-2's procedures, and then names every interval with the analysis's own rules. What is not known is swept: how much easier distant targets are than close ones (lambda), how good a repair is (rho_c), including a repair that does worse than a fresh sample, and how often each kind of candidate passes its trial (SIZE:17–25; ruling 72, point 4).

The rule. N is the smallest number of blocks at which each primary interval is resolved, meaning not unresolved, in at least 80% of simulated runs in every combination swept [PICKED 2] (SIZE:61). A run in which a pooled step is named task-dependent counts as resolved, because that name is a definite result. The share is counted in whole runs (ruling 84, point 5). The 80% is the chance for one interval taken alone, under the simulation's assumptions. It is not the chance that all three resolve (DESIGN:153).

Why the simulation was corrected. The version of 11 November gave N = 32. Its two weakest intervals, D − A on distant targets in reach and in land, resolved in 83.05% and 83.15% of runs in their worst combination (`notes/SAMPLE-SIZE-RERUN-20261111.md`; `reproduction/ladder-pooled3-20261111.json`). Its adoption side was fitted to scored-2, whose repair showed the model no procedure's flights: in all 33 flights flown again after a failed test in scored-2's 48 learning runs, `procedure_steps` is 0 (`run/episodes.json`). It drew repairs and first candidates from the same pools, so a repair passed about as often as a first candidate, and it had arm A adopt its first candidate in every block. The second development sample contradicted both. Its first and fresh candidates passed their zone trial in 2 of 44 slots and its repairs in 17 of 34, and arm A adopted c0 in 9 of 12 blocks. One count, the blocks in which arm D adopted its first candidate, was 0, outside the full range of 2 to 12 that the simulation allowed. By section 8.4's rule the simulation was corrected and N chosen again (ruling 72, points 2 and 3). The files of 11 November stay as they were written.

The correction. Only the adoption side changes. The evaluation side was not contradicted.

- A candidate passes its zone trial with a probability set by its kind: pi_f for a first or fresh candidate and pi_r for a repair. The same rates hold for every task, since 12 blocks cannot separate them by task.
- Arm A's three candidates, c0 and the two fresh ones, each pass its zone-free trial with probability pi_A, and A adopts the first that passes, or nothing. Arms A and B share one stream of fresh candidates, one model call for each (ruling 77, points 1 and 2).
- An adopted candidate's evaluation outcomes are drawn once, whichever arms adopt it. If any arm adopted it after passing a zone trial, the pool it comes from and the procedure within that pool are drawn together, weighted by the procedure's chance of passing that trial. The two pools are scored-2's procedures learned with the zone and without it. Passing a trial therefore still selects the better procedures, and the better pool, as it did on 11 November. A repair that keeps c0's behaviour, which the sweep allows when rho_c is below 0, is drawn the same way. If only arm A adopted the candidate, after its zone-free trial, the draw is unweighted, as the 11 November model drew A's c0 (ruling 77, point 3; ruling 85, points 2 and 3). No draw had every weight 0.
- Each of the three rates takes three values: the two ends of the two-sided 95% Clopper-Pearson interval of its count in the sample, and the count's own share. They are 0.0056, 0.0455 and 0.1547 for pi_f (2 of 44), 0.3243, 0.5000 and 0.6757 for pi_r (17 of 34), and 0.4281, 0.7500 and 0.9451 for pi_A (9 of 12). With the 12 combinations of lambda and rho_c of 11 November that makes 324 combinations. pi_A is counted from arm A's c0, which passed its zone-free trial in 9 of 12 blocks (ruling 72, point 3). Counting also the first fresh candidates, which passed in the 3 blocks where c0 failed, would give 12 of 15, a share of 0.80, which lies inside the swept interval. The sweep over the Clopper-Pearson interval is how the protocol answers its own warning that four blocks cannot estimate a rate. No interval or step estimate of the sample enters the model. Its evaluation rows are used only in the acceptance check below.
- The pools, scenarios, margin, classifier and the 90% and 95% intervals are as on 11 November.

The first build of this correction had three faults, which an independent review found after its tables had been read and before any choice was made from them. A repair that kept c0 had c0's outcomes drawn unweighted, the pool of c1 and of each repair was chosen before the trial rather than with the procedure, and c1 was counted as two model calls. They were fixed, the fix was reviewed, and the simulation was run afresh (rulings 85 and 86). The faulty build's tables, also of 19 November, are kept on record and superseded (`notes/SAMPLE-SIZE-RERUN-20261119.md`). Every figure below is from the fresh run.

That table holds 5,000 simulated samples of 4 blocks per task for each of the 324 combinations (`reproduction/ladder-devgrid-20261119b.json`). Every count lay inside its central 95% range, so none lay outside its full range. Arm D's first candidate adopted, 0 of 12, which lay outside 2 to 12 on 11 November, now has a central range of 0 to 5 (`notes/SAMPLE-SIZE-TABLES-20261119b.md`, Table A; ruling 86, point 3).

### 6.7 The gate

Before any outcome is computed, the gate checks the whole run and fails closed. It derives the schedule from the seeds file: 3 × 40 = 120 learning blocks, then the evaluation and retention phases implied by which sources were adopted. It takes the three task roots as one run (section 4.5). It requires that:

- every scheduled phase is present with its done mark, and there is no extra phase;
- every phase holds exactly its scheduled scenarios, each once, with the scheduled parameters;
- every hash-chained log checks against its heads file, and every result agrees with its flight's own record and log;
- every success agrees with the simulator's true positions and speeds;
- every flight it counts was flown in real time with the fixed delay, its run configuration naming three periods and its outcome 0.3 s, and is on time by `ladder.timing` (section 4.6). The one exception is a flight kept late. A learning flight kept late is accepted only beside exactly two set-aside attempts of the same call. An evaluation flight kept late is accepted only in a phase with exactly two earlier attempts set aside as late, each with a receipt for lateness, beside any attempts set aside as crashed, and only for a case the phase schedules. Every set-aside attempt is checked against its own protected episode log;
- each evaluation phase flew its cases in the order its instance fixes (`flight_order` in `protocol/contract3.py`);
- each phase's platform is recorded as section 4.7 says, and a recorded nice value other than 0 is refused;
- each phase names its platform's sandbox route, and a cluster phase names its kernel release and has a `sandbox-end.json` in which no holder was replaced for a reason other than 'stopped' or 'residue', and none had died (section 4.8);
- within a block, the candidates, requests, trials and adoption agree with `arms.json`, and a fresh request is identical to c0's;
- a first request shows only its own first-round flights, and a repair shows only trial flights of its own arm, with at least one from the last trial flown, or, for a repair made before any trial of its arm flew, its arm's first-round flights (section 2.2);
- each trial passed exactly when all 3 of its layouts passed the trial rule, as the gate recomputes it from each flight's protected decision log;
- a retention phase's roles are worked out as the driver works them out, so a phase that flies c0's own source because an arm adopted c0 is accepted;
- every model call of a scored run is a real call with a served-model label, all calls requested the same settings, and no message id appears twice. A row logged as a copy is accepted only in a learning attempt that was set aside. On the gateway route every provider row's id is in the run's file of ids. Every line of that file has exactly the keys `message_id` and `block`, apart from a line cut off by a killed process, which is counted and reported. Provider rows and spending rows pair one to one by request id. An unpaired provider row in a block that was set aside is counted and its cost added to the reported spend of set-aside blocks, and in a block that was kept it is refused;
- no request contains a seed number of either seed book: evaluation, retention and learning;
- a format repair follows only a reply with text, and a slot that failed the format with one call had an empty reply;
- all phases share one protocol hash, one seeds hash, one wheel, one source fingerprint and one set of model settings, and one platform with one partition and processor model;
- all phases share one sandbox route and one number flying at once, and no scored phase ran with uncommitted source;
- when it is not judging a pilot, the run's values equal those of `protocol/FROZEN-3.json` (section 9.2), and the gate refuses if the file is absent;
- every session's spending stop was set by `--spend-limit` and is at most US$20, and it reports them all.

A learning block that finished with an arm adopting nothing is a completed non-adoption and supplies planned zeros. A block that is missing or stopped is a fault, never a zero. If any check fails, the analysis computes no outcome.

Operating rule: nobody reads flight outcomes before the gate passes on the complete run. Phase logs print outcomes as flights land, so for the logs this is a rule and not something the code enforces. It binds every agent that operates or reviews the study, and Simon. The printing of a block's adoptions happens only after the check that can stop the block (ruling 39, point 4).

The protocol states no prediction for the direction or size of any step. The simulation's ranges are planning inputs and not predictions to be confirmed.

## 7. Faults, late flights and reruns

Two kinds of trouble can stop a phase from finishing cleanly. A fault is a failure of the machine, the sandbox's route or the model service. A late flight is a flight whose decisions missed the deadline of section 4.6. They are handled by different rules, and neither is ever charged to a procedure except where section 4.6 says so.

### 7.1 Faults

A fault is a failure of the machine, of the sandbox's route or of the model service, and never of a procedure. On the machine's side the faults are that the simulator failed to start, the connection to it dropped, the simulator's true state was missing, the runner crashed or the sandbox's route failed and raised `RouteError` (section 4.8). On the model's side, a call failed in transport, timed out, was refused by the gateway, came back with no served-model label or was a copy of a reply already seen (section 2.2). A phase with a fault does not get its done mark. The scheduler holds it and lists it and goes on to the phases after it.

Every learning block that stopped on a fault is set aside and flown again under the same name and seeds, by this rule and with no choice made by anyone. The rule has two limits.

- Before a block is set aside for a fault, the free key check runs. If the gateway refuses the key, nothing is set aside, the job stops with its own status, and Simon is told. On 16 November the gateway refused the key at the first call of every block of the first development sample, each block stopped as a fault, and nothing was spent (section 8.4).
- A block may be set aside for a fault at most twice, the same number of attempts as a late call. A third fault in the same block stops the job with its own status, for Simon. A set-aside because the batch job died is not a fault of the block and does not count (ruling 40, point 5).

The number of blocks set aside, by kind of fault and HTTP status, is reported. A block's fault is read from the end of its provider log, which is the only route to its kind or status (ruling 46, point 4). A block whose own request content provokes a fault is in effect dropped selectively, because the same content provokes it again. Any fault, and a job's end, is also more likely to fall on a block where c0 failed, because that block makes more calls and flies more trials. Section 6.2 lists such blocks and bounds their effect, and section 10 names both as limits (ruling 81).

Which blocks section 6.2 lists, and how they are counted, is fixed by ruling 87. A block is listed if a set-aside attempt had sent a request for a fresh candidate or a repair. A request file written for such a slot with no usage or provider row counts as sent, and is marked 'sending not established', because a job killed during the call leaves exactly that. Only the names of the candidate folders are read, never a request's contents. An attempt whose candidate folders were not kept is listed for all four arms of its block and marked 'not determinable'. The paper gives the set-aside attempts by reason, the distinct blocks with any set-aside attempt and the distinct listed blocks, each named as what it counts, and reports a list it could not complete as incomplete (section 6.2).

### 7.2 Late flights

Lateness is decided from the timing fields of a flight, by the rule of section 4.6, and from nothing else.

In a learning block the driver checks each call's flights as they land. A call with a late flight is set aside unread and flown again in place, at most twice, with no model call. A third late attempt is kept. A trial's is charged to its candidate. A first-round call's has no procedure to charge, and it is counted with arms A, B and C for the matched list (P0) and with arm D for the threat list (P1) (section 4.1). The block does not stop.

An evaluation phase follows the same rule. A phase with a late flight is set aside and flown again, at most twice. The third attempt is kept whatever its timing. Its flights count as flown with the outcomes recorded, and each late one is marked kept late. After two flights again the lateness is charged to the procedure, as it is in learning. The gate accepts a kept-late evaluation flight only in a phase with exactly two earlier attempts set aside as late, each with a receipt for lateness, beside any set aside as crashed (section 6.7). The driver refuses to fly a phase that already has more than two attempts set aside for lateness, with a status and a line of its own, so an inconsistent history stops and is seen. The analysis reports the kept-late evaluation flights by arm and bounds them (section 6.2). No person decides anything at a late evaluation phase (ruling 39, point 2; ruling 45, point 3).

### 7.3 Setting a phase aside and flying it again

The unit is the whole phase. One flight is never replaced. A phase is set aside with the set-aside tool, `protocol/set_aside3.py`, for one of two reasons.

- `late`. A flight in an evaluation phase was late and was not kept. The tool opens the phase's `timing.json`, copies only the named timing fields of each flight into the receipt (`TIMING_FIELDS`), and refuses unless a flight is recorded late. Where the phase has a `sandbox-end.json`, which is the route's record and holds no outcome, the tool reads it too, and then reads the platform from the phase's manifest to judge that record. A phase with no route record is set aside without its manifest being opened. A learning block is never set aside as late, because section 4.1 flies a late call again inside the block and keeps no block out for lateness.
- `crashed`. Any other fault of section 7.1 that left the folder without a done mark. The tool accepts it only with evidence. The evidence is that the scheduler recorded a failed exit for the phase. Or it is that the scheduler which started the phase is no longer running and its log has the phase's start with no end, which is a job killed by its time limit or a lost node. To make that judgement the tool reads the scheduler's log lines for the phase. For an evaluation phase it also opens `timing.json` for the late flags alone, and it refuses `crashed` for an evaluation phase that has a late flight not marked kept late, and says to use `late`. An evaluation phase whose third attempt was kept late and whose job then died before the done mark is set aside as `crashed` and flown again, with its two late attempts still counted. It never opens an outcome, a flight record, `arms.json` or the phase log. A crashed learning block's receipt lists `timing.json` among the files read although the tool no longer opens it. That is a wrong record, and the paper's supplement reports it if a learning block is set aside as crashed in the scored run (ruling 57, point 3).

The refusal of a phase whose route record the gate would refuse applies to both reasons. The tool refuses a phase that has a done mark, so a finished phase cannot be removed once its result exists. It refuses while a scheduler is running on the run, and while any process still names the phase's folder. It writes a receipt: the phase, the attempt number, the reason, the time, the timing fields it read, the SHA-256 of `timing.json` and every file it opened in the phase, in order. It moves the phase's folder and its log to a folder beside the run root, `<run>.set-aside/<phase>-attempt<n>`, where they are kept. This is the second protocol's treatment of a crashed phase (`PROTOCOL-2.md`:219–221).

Who sets aside. In the scored run the batch scripts do it, through one rule, `protocol/phase_kind3.py`, which gives `late` for an evaluation phase with a late row not marked kept late and `crashed` for anything else. The same rule governs a resume after a job died (section 4.5). A script stops, rather than setting aside, when a receipt is incomplete, when the tool refuses or when a phase has been set aside twice already for the reason it is now held for. A set-aside made by a resume after the batch job died, whose receipt's note begins "resume:", is not counted (`count_aside` in `cluster/scored.sbatch`; section 7.1). The two reasons are counted apart, so an evaluation phase can be set aside twice as `late` and twice as `crashed`. No phase is set aside by hand during the rehearsal or the scored run with a reason other than the one `phase_kind3.py` prints for it (ruling 57, point 2). Every set-aside made by hand is recorded in a ruling with that output.

The phase is then run again under its scheduled name with the same seeds. A learning block makes new model calls. The gate sees one folder for each scheduled phase. The paper lists every phase set aside, with its receipt, and the model calls of a block set aside are counted in the spend. Each is also entered in the deviations file.

### 7.4 The gate has no exception path

If a phase cannot be completed under these rules, the study has no gated result. Simon decides what follows.

A partial run is not analysed as this study's result, whatever its size. If Simon asks for a reading of an incomplete run, it is a deviation analysis and is labelled as one. It uses the leading block numbers that are complete for all three tasks, as the schedule of section 4.5 fixes them, with no choice by anyone. It is not given the names of section 6.4. Such a reading is unbiased only if the stop did not depend on outcomes, and the paper would say that this is assumed.

### 8.5 Timing preflights and diagnostics, 11 and 12 November, no model

These were flown on the Mac before the fixed delay existed. They matter now for two reasons: they set the Mac's load limit, and they showed why lateness had to be counted from the observation and not from the schedule.

How the preflight works (`protocol/timing_preflight3.py`). On a machine left to itself, the development evaluation and retention scenarios of all three tasks are flown in real time with each task's reference controller. Each task flies 10 flights one at a time, then 40 on instances 10 to 13 together. In the four-at-once stage each simulator flies its scenarios in its own order and the four start a quarter of a second apart, so that no two fly the same scenario at the same moment, as in the scored run. If every flight is on time, the limit is the highest one-minute load average at the start of any four-at-once flight. If any flight is late there is no limit, the machine is made quieter and the preflight is flown again. The first two preflights flew the same scenario on all four simulators together. The four decisions of every second then started their sandboxed processes at the same instant, and that alone took 42.5% of them past one step (`reproduction/timing-diagnostic-sandbox-20261112-082315.json`).

What the five runs showed.

- The first preflight stopped after 108 flights when macOS installed an update and restarted the machine. The second finished and had four four-at-once flights late. The third flew complete from 10:23 to 11:37 am on 12 November and had four late flights, all in the four-at-once stage of reach. All of that stage's host lag fell in three windows of 20 to 40 s, two of them with desktop use and git commands on the Mac. None gave a limit.
- A diagnostic that gave each simulator its own order and a quarter-second start had host lag at four of 432 decisions after the first, against 11.7% and 38.8% when the same stage was flown together (`reproduction/timing-diagnostic-rotated-20261112-085816.json`).
- The fourth preflight was flown at night from 9:44 to 10:58 pm with nobody at the Mac and no agent running. None of its 150 flights was late. It gives the load limit of 4.89, the highest one-minute start load of a four-at-once flight (`reproduction/timing-preflight-flights-20261112-214408.json`; round two, ruling 4). Start load did not separate late flights from on-time ones in the first three preflights. The limit therefore guards only against sustained overload, such as the loads of 6.5 and more on 9 November, and cannot catch a burst of tens of seconds. It applies on the Mac only.

The rule of 12 November. Draft 1's rule is kept as `timing_before_delay` in `ardupilot/ladder.py` for reading records made before 13 November. Under it a flight was late if a lag was missing or not finite, or if any command was issued 1 s or more after its schedule. It was also late if the median of its lags, each read on the telemetry step, was above half a step, 0.05 s. It counted every lag from the step's schedule, and a recorded lag of one step then had two sources. Every decision runs the procedure and the guard check as two new sandboxed processes, and these took a median of 0.072 s in scored-2's hazard flights, so the turnaround sits just below the 0.1 s step. A host lag arises when the turnaround crosses the next stamp. A clock offset arises when the stamp nearest the scheduled time falls a millisecond or two before it, so the runner waits for the next stamp and the procedure is given an observation already about 0.1 s past its schedule. Under the fixed delay a clock offset is the observation's own lag and does not count against the decision (section 4.6).

Why the owner's first rule was revised. It took the median of the recorded lag. A flight with half its decisions one step late had a median between 0.049 and 0.0505 s, so its verdict turned on a millisecond of jitter. In the first two preflights, the flights the first rule called late flew within 0.26 m of on-time flights of the same seed at every step, and ended as they did. On-time flights of one seed differed among themselves by up to 2.28 m.

Scored-2 under the rule of 12 November. None of its 920 hazard flights and 213 first-round learning flights is late. Its 213 trial flights have 7 late, and its 3,125 held-out, disturbance and repeat flights have 124 late. Every one of those 124 was flown between 9 am and 6 pm on 5 November 2026. None of the 3,347 evaluation flights that a procedure flew between 6 pm and 9 am was late (`reproduction/timing-preflight-20261111.json`; the owner's read-only counts of 12 November in the note). Read for own lag, 15 of scored-2's 150,379 decisions had an own lag of two steps or more, in 11 of 4,258 flights (round four, 'What the records show', point 4).

## 10. What this protocol cannot show

- It cannot say what the model's revision adds apart from the evidence in the failed zone flights. C − B carries both.
- It cannot say what seeing the zone adds without testing against it. That arm does not exist, and the interactions between the parts are not estimated.
- Every arm is told that zones may appear, so the study cannot say what telling the model about zones adds.
- On a task where arm B rarely adopts, it cannot compare revision with a working alternative. There, C − B is arm C's success, and B − A is the cost of a strict test.
- A pooled step can hide task effects that point in opposite directions. The per-task estimates are printed for that reason, and they are wide, and section 6.4 says how the pooled name and the per-task signs are read together.
- It cannot attribute a difference between bands to clearance, because mission length changes with it. RQ4 is asked of the distance bands for that reason.
- It cannot show that learning with the zone costs nothing in ordinary flight. Retention has 10 scenarios and no margin.
- 'No gain or loss as large as the margin' is a statement about 0.20, a planning assumption. It is not a statement that an effect is absent, or that 0.20 is the size that matters.
- The three primary intervals are pointwise. Under the corrected simulation's assumptions, at 40 blocks each resolves in at least 91.8% of simulated runs in every combination swept, and there is no assurance that all three will. For pooled B − A that figure holds because 'task-dependent' counts as resolved. Counted the other way, its lowest is 76.1% at 40 blocks (section 6.6).
- RQ4 is answered by six secondary intervals, and the study cannot promise to resolve them. At 40 blocks five of the six miss the planning target, 80% of runs resolved, in some of the simulation's settings, and resolve in only 19.1% to 27.8% of simulated runs in the worst. Only 61 of the 324 settings have four or more of the six each more likely unresolved than resolved, 139 have none, and the settings carry no probabilities (ruling 96, point 2). So the study may not be able to say, for a given task, whether the total gain holds for distant targets. An unresolved interval there is not evidence that arms D and A do not differ (sections 6.3 and 6.6).
- If two arms have identical outcomes, or no arm succeeds, or every flight succeeds, the bootstrap has nothing to resample and the study can say only what section 6.4 names. For identical outcomes the one-sided 95% bound on the share of blocks where the arms would differ is 1 − 0.05^(1/N) with N the number of blocks compared. That is 7.2% for one task's 40 blocks and 2.5% for a pooled step's 120, so the study cannot rule out a difference in a smaller share of blocks than that.
- The results hold for one model, one learning core, three tasks, one kind of hazard and simulated flight. The model label cannot reveal a substituted model.
- Blocks come from one served model over the days of the run. If the service changes the model between sittings, the labels may not show it.
- Procedures do not perceive the zone. It arrives as coordinates. The zone is tested once per 1 s decision step, at the step's end (`ardupilot/pack.py`:496–502), as in the second protocol, so entering it means being inside at one of those tests.
- The same flight does not always end the same way. One scripted case ended two ways at 0.3 s on different nodes. One of 54 paired scripted trial outcomes differed between the rehearsal of job 4802495 and the two pilots of 18 November, with the same procedures and every command sent on its stamp (ruling 74, point 4). The study's coupling of arms that adopted the same source rests on a rule fixed in advance (section 4.2). It does not assume that the simulator repeats a flight exactly.
- Every command reaches the vehicle 0.3 s after its observation, and scored-2 sent each command as soon as it was decided. A comparison of the two studies' flights carries that difference, and the paper says so (round four, ruling 6). The requests also differ: this protocol adds the procedure-only sentence and the sentence about the delay, and its repairs show different flights (section 2.2).
- The planning simulation was first fitted to scored-2's adoption rates, from flights without the delay, and its rescue rates came from a repair that showed no procedure's flights. The second development sample put one count outside its full range, and the simulation was corrected so that its pass rates come from that sample. Every count of the sample then lay inside its range (section 6.6). Those rates rest on 12 blocks, so whether the scored run's procedures adopt at rates inside the corrected ranges is known only when it has flown (section 8.4).
- Blinding is not enforced. Operators who run the study can see adoption counts in the scheduler's log and the spend, and a failed block's log, as section 4.5 says. The rules of sections 7.1 and 7.3 remove the decisions they could make on that. A fault that comes from a request's own content would drop that block selectively, because the same content provokes it again. The count of blocks set aside by kind and status is reported.
- A learning block flown again after a fault or a job's end is drawn again, and a block where c0 failed is more likely to be flown again because it makes more calls and flies more trials. That would pull B − A and C − B towards zero. Section 6.2 lists such blocks and fills them both ways as a sensitivity analysis; it cannot say what the lost attempt would have shown (ruling 81).
- Every flight of record flies on one node type, the L40S nodes of one cluster. Nothing flown there stands for another node type or for the Mac, and a call's waiting time is not comparable across platforms (ruling 22; round four, ruling 6).
- The nodes are shared with other users' jobs on other cores. The time of a sandboxed call moves by a few per cent with the node's other work, enough to change how many decisions have one step of own lag. In the development sample no decision missed the deadline, and procedures written by a model used at most two of the three steps (section 8.4). The position test, which compares readiness early and late in a block, failed in three of the four scripted pilots on the source of record and in one block of the sample. The records explain it by what the thirds of a block hold and by clock offsets, and cannot name a single cause for the part due to readiness. It is reported as a diagnostic (section 8.4).
- Timing can still change a result through a missed deadline, a call flown again or a flight kept late, though every on-time command is sent at the same stamp. Kept-late evaluation flights and learning calls kept late are bounded by the fills of section 6.2. Those are sensitivity analyses, and the primary estimates are unchanged by them.
- A scored run's spend can pass the stops, since blocks in flight finish after a stop trips, and the gateway charges 1.10 times the list price the stops count (section 4.5).
